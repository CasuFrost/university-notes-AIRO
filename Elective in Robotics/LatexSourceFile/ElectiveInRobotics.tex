\part{Modeling and Control of Multi-Rotor UAVs}
\chapter{Introduction}
UAV stands for \textbf{Unmanned Aerial Vehicle}, also known by other names depending on the domain, like \textit{RPVs (Remotely Piloted Vehicles)} in the military domain, or \textit{Drones} in common parlance. We will use all three names interchangeably. The methods presented are valid also for remotely operated or autonomous vehicles (at different levels of autonomy).\bigskip

The term UAVs refers to a broad category of vehicles; in general, any aerial vehicle without a pilot on board is a UAV. We will examine UAVs composed of multiple rotors, in particular \textbf{quadrotors}.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth ]{images/design based classification.png}
    \caption{Design-based classification.}
\end{figure}
\noindent Quadrotors are used in various situations:\begin{itemize}
    \item inspection, maintenance,
    \item communication networks,
    \item search and rescue operations,
    \item aerial transportation, manipulation.
\end{itemize}
These activities require a vertical, stationary and slow flight. A quadrotor is characterized by high maneuverability, can remain suspended in the air without moving (hovering), and can perform a \textit{vertical take-off and landing (VTOL)}, unlike other vehicles such as airplanes, which require a horizontal movement in order to take off and land.
\section{Quadrotor Modeling}
We call VTOLs the vehicles that can perform a vertical take-off and landing; these are modeled with the North-East-Down (NED) reference frame:
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth ]{images/NEDframe.eps}
    \caption{The NED reference frame.}
    \label{NED}
\end{figure}
\noindent This frame, often called $SR_i$, has the $\mathbf z$ axis pointing down to the center of the Earth. According to the dynamics of rigid bodies given by the Newton-Euler formalism, the translational dynamics of a body in the $SR_i$ frame is given by\begin{equation}\label{sum_forces}
    \sum_j \mathbf F_j = m\dot {\mathbf V}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf F_j$ are the external forces applied to the center of mass, and $\dot {\mathbf V}$ is the acceleration of the center of mass expressed in $SR_i$. The rotational dynamics is given by\begin{equation}\label{sum_moments}
     \sum_j \mathbf  M_j = J\dot{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} + \boldsymbol{\Omega} \times J \boldsymbol{\Omega}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf M_j$ are the external moments acting on the center of mass, $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ is the angular velocity of the body expressed in the body frame, denoted $SR_b$, and $J$ is the inertia tensor, also expressed in $SR_b$.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth ]{images/bodyframe.pdf}
    \caption{$SR_b$ is the reference frame attached to the center of mass of the vehicle, $R$ is the rotation matrix between the two frames.}
    \label{bodyframe}
\end{figure}
\noindent A more explicit expression of the forces in Equation \eqref{sum_forces} is the following:\begin{equation}
    m\dot {\mathbf V} =  -T R\mathbf z
    +\mathbf F_e(\mathbf V,\dot{\mathbf V},\boldsymbol{\Omega},\dot{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\mathbf d(t))
    +R\Sigma_R\btau+mg\mathbf z
\end{equation}
where:\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathbf d(t)$ are the external disturbances that do not depend on 
    the vehicle's state.
    \item $\mathbf F_e$ are the external forces, which depend on the state.
    \item $T$ is the \textbf{thrust}, which depends on the number and allocation of the rotors, while $-\mathbf z$ is the direction pointing upward (according to the NED frame as in Figure \ref{NED}). $R$ is the rotation matrix that describes the orientation of our vehicle with respect to the fixed NED frame $SR_i$.
    \item Hence, the product $-T R\mathbf z$ is the main upward thrust.
    \item $\btau$ is the torque generated by the propellers.
    \item $R\Sigma_R\btau$ are the parasitic forces generated by the rotors/actuators. The coupling matrix $\Sigma_R$ maps the torques/rotations of the motors into small translational lateral forces (then rotated into $SR_i$ via $R$).
\end{itemize}
We know that the derivative of the rotation matrix $R$ depends on the skew-symmetric matrix of the angular velocity:\begin{equation}
    \dot R= R\cdot S(\boldsymbol{\Omega})
\end{equation}
$S(\boldsymbol{\Omega})$ is important since it appears in the explicit expression of the term $ J\dot{\boldsymbol{\Omega}} $ in Equation \eqref{sum_moments}:
\begin{equation}
    J\dot{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}=-S(\boldsymbol{\Omega})J\boldsymbol{\Omega}+\btau+
    \btau_e(\mathbf V,\dot{\mathbf V},\boldsymbol{\Omega},\dot{\boldsymbol{\Omega}},\mathbf d(t))+ \btau_g+ 
    \Sigma_T T \mathbf z
\end{equation}
where:\begin{itemize}
    \item $\btau_e$ are the torques induced by the external forces.
    \item $\Sigma_T T \mathbf z$ is a parasitic moment. $\Sigma_T$ is a matrix that represents the geometric misalignment between the effective axis of the total thrust $T$ and the center of mass of the vehicle. If the thrust vector does not pass exactly through the center of gravity, it generates a parasitic torque proportional to the thrust itself.
    \item $\btau_g$ is the internal gyroscopic moment due to the inertia of the propellers rotating at high speed (particularly relevant in quadrotors when they change attitude rapidly).
\end{itemize}
We denote $\vec{\mathbf T}=-T\mathbf z_b$ the push in the opposite direction of $\mathbf z_b$ expressed in the vehicle frame $SR_b$. 
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.2\textwidth ]{images/thrust.pdf}
\end{figure}
\noindent A quadrotor is a UAV with four motors located at the extremities of a cross-shaped frame (Figure \ref{quadrotor}) and is controlled by imposing a desired angular speed on each rotor.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth ]{images/quadrotor.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic of a quadrotor.}
    \label{quadrotor}
\end{figure}
Let's identify a disc on each rotor, which follows the profile of the propellers: we denote by $f_i$ the scalar force on each propeller, which is perpendicular to the disc; its value is:\begin{equation}\label{f_i}
    f_i=b\omega_i^2
\end{equation}
where $\omega_i$ is the angular speed of the $i$-th propeller, and $b$ is a constant \textit{thrust factor} that depends on the rotor geometry and profile, and on air density. The rotational speed of each blade is controlled by a low-level controller. 

We denote $\tau_{R,i}$  the drag torque generated by the $i$-th rotor as it spins in the air:\begin{equation}\label{tau_R}
    \tau_{R,i}=d\omega_i^2
\end{equation}
where $d$ is a \textit{drag factor} that, like the thrust factor, depends on the rotor.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth ]{images/force_distribution.eps}
\end{figure}
We denote $\varphi,\vartheta,\psi$ the roll-pitch-yaw angles of the vehicle with respect to the fixed reference frame $SR_i$, hence the already mentioned rotation matrix is:\begin{equation}
    {}^iR_b=\begin{pmatrix}
        \cos\psi\cos\vartheta& \cos\psi\sin\vartheta\sin\varphi-\sin\psi\cos\varphi&\cos\psi\sin\vartheta\cos\varphi+\sin\psi\sin\varphi\\
        \sin\psi\cos\vartheta& \sin\psi\sin\vartheta\sin\varphi+\cos\psi\cos\varphi&  \sin\psi\sin\vartheta\cos\varphi-\sin\varphi\cos\psi\\
        -\sin\vartheta&\cos\vartheta\sin\varphi&\cos\vartheta\cos\varphi
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth ]{images/configuration.eps}
    \caption{Configuration of the quadrotor.}
    \label{configuration}
\end{figure}
The linear velocity $\mathbf V$ of our quadrotor is expressed in $SR_i$ while the angular velocity $\boldsymbol{\Omega}$ is expressed in $SR_b$, we can make the reference system explicit using superscripts as follows:\begin{equation}
    {}^i\mathbf V=\begin{pmatrix}
    v_x\\v_y\\v_z
    \end{pmatrix},\quad {}^b\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\begin{pmatrix}
        p\\ q\\ r
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Let $\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3,\omega_4$  be our control inputs (the velocity of the rotors), $f_i$ and $\tau_{R,i}$ are derived from these quantities as in Equations \eqref{f_i} and \eqref{tau_R}. The total thrust will be:\begin{equation}
    T=f_1+f_2+f_3+f_4.
\end{equation}
We recall that on the quadrotor, a force $\vec{\mathbf T}=-T\mathbf z_b$ acts in the same direction as $\mathbf z_b$ but with the opposite sense. Intuitively, if the forces $f_i$ are identical, we can expect the quadrotor to accelerate towards $-\mathbf z_b$ without rotating on itself. If the forces are not identical, they may cause a rotation of the body. The torques generated on the axes $\mathbf x_b,\mathbf y_b,\mathbf z_b$ are the following:\begin{align}
    &\tau_\varphi=l(f_2-f_4),\\ 
    &\tau_\vartheta=l(f_1-f_3),\\
    &\tau_\psi=-\tau_{R,1}+\tau_{R,2}-\tau_{R,3}+\tau_{R,4}.
\end{align}
$l$ represents the arm length (or lever arm) of the quadrotor, as shown in Figure \ref{configuration}.\bigskip

\noindent For simplicity, we can assume that the inertia matrix shown in Equation \eqref{sum_moments} is diagonal:\begin{equation}
    J=\begin{pmatrix}
        I_x&0&0\\
        0&I_y&0\\
        0&0&I_z
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
the angular velocity instead can be expressed as a function of the roll-pitch-yaw angles:\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\Omega}=\begin{pmatrix}
        p\\ q\\ r
    \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}
        1&0&-\sin\vartheta\\ 
        0&\cos\varphi&\cos\vartheta\sin\varphi\\ 
        0&-\sin\varphi&\cos\vartheta\cos\varphi
    \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}
        \dot\varphi\\ \dot\vartheta\\ \dot\psi
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Let's see in detail what the applied forces and moments are. We write explicitly the sum of forces of the translational dynamics (that we recall is expressed in the fixed frame $SR_i$):\begin{equation}
    \sum_j \mathbf F_j=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}
        0\\0\\mg
    \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{weight}}
    +
    \underbrace{{}^iR_b\begin{pmatrix}
        0\\0\\-T
    \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{actuation}} + \mathbf F_A+\mathbf F_D
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf F_A$ are the aerodynamics-related forces, which mainly include the aerodynamic resistance of the body and arms of the aircraft to advancement, as well as any parasitic lift effects generated by the shape of the drone or by the rotor discs in translation, while $\mathbf F_D$ are the disturbances (for example, wind).

The sum of moments is the following:\begin{equation}
    \sum_j \mathbf M_j=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}
        L_\varphi\\L_\vartheta\\L_\psi
    \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{gyroscopic effects}}+
     \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}
     \tau_\varphi\\\tau_\vartheta\\\tau_\psi
     \end{pmatrix}}_{\text{actuation}} +\btau_A+\btau_D.
\end{equation}
$\btau_A$ is the resulting torque induced by the distributed aerodynamic forces when they do not have an application point coinciding with the center of gravity of the vehicle, combined with the rotational drag that the air exerts against the rotations of the rigid body in roll, pitch and yaw. $\btau_D$ is the moment produced by the disturbances.

\begin{boxdefinition}{State of the Quadrotor}{}
Given the previous premises, we define the state of a quadrotor as a vector in $\R^{12}$ defined as follows:\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\xi}=(
        x,\quad y,\quad z,\quad v_x,\quad v_y,\quad v_z,\quad \varphi ,\quad  \vartheta ,\quad  \psi,\quad  p ,\quad q,\quad r
    )^\top
\end{equation}
Here $x,y,z$ are the coordinates of the position of the center of mass of the quadrotor expressed in $SR_i$, $v_x,v_y,v_z$ its velocity, $\varphi ,  \vartheta ,  \psi$ the roll-pitch-yaw angles between $SR_i$ and $SR_b$, and $p,q,r$ the angular velocity of the quadrotor expressed in $SR_b$.
\end{boxdefinition}
\noindent The evolution in time of the quadrotor state is given by a dynamical system of the following form:\begin{equation}\label{quadrotorSystem}
    \dot{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=f(\boldsymbol{\xi})+g(\boldsymbol{\xi})\mathbf u 
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf u$ are the inputs of the system defined as follows:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{u}=\begin{pmatrix}T\\ \tau_\varphi \\  \tau_\vartheta \\  \tau_\psi
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
these quantities depend on $f_i,\tau_{R,i}$, which in turn depend on the angular velocity of the rotors $\omega_i$, which we assume we can control perfectly. 
\begin{boxdefinition}{Time Evolution of the Quadrotor}{time_ev}
Let $\Omega_r$ be the the signed residual speed of the blades
$$
\Omega_r=-\omega_1+\omega_2-\omega_3+\omega_4
$$
and $I_r$ the inertia of the blades; the explicit expression of the system in Equation \eqref{quadrotorSystem} is the following:
\begin{align}
& \dot{x} = v_x \\
& \dot{y} = v_y \\
& \dot{z} = v_z \\
& \dot{v}_x = F_{A,x} - (\cos(\psi) \sin(\vartheta) \cos(\varphi) + \sin(\psi) \sin(\varphi)) \frac{T}{m} \\
& \dot{v}_y = F_{A,y} - (\sin(\psi) \sin(\vartheta) \cos(\varphi) - \sin(\varphi) \cos(\psi)) \frac{T}{m} \\
& \dot{v}_z = F_{A,z} + g - \cos(\vartheta) \cos(\varphi) \frac{T}{m} \\
& \dot{\varphi} = p + \sin(\varphi) \tan(\vartheta) q + \cos(\varphi) \tan(\vartheta) r \\
& \dot{\vartheta} = \cos(\varphi) q - \sin(\varphi) r \\
& \dot{\psi} = \sin(\varphi) \sec(\vartheta) q + \cos(\varphi) \sec(\vartheta) r \\
& \dot{p} = \tau_{A,x} + \frac{I_r}{I_x} q \Omega_r + \frac{I_y - I_z}{I_x} q r + \frac{\tau_\varphi}{I_x} \\
& \dot{q} = \tau_{A,y} - \frac{I_r}{I_y} p \Omega_r + \frac{I_z - I_x}{I_y} p r + \frac{\tau_\vartheta}{I_y} \\
& \dot{r} = \tau_{A,z} + \frac{I_x - I_y}{I_z} p q + \frac{\tau_\psi}{I_z}.
\end{align}
\end{boxdefinition}
\section{Quadrotor Control}
The model in Definition \ref{def:time_ev} is too complex for control purposes. We neglect aerodynamics and gyroscopic effects, and we assume small variations of $\varphi$ and $\vartheta$ such that\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \dot\varphi&\dot\vartheta&\dot\psi
    \end{pmatrix}^\top\simeq \begin{pmatrix}
        p&q&r
    \end{pmatrix}^\top
\end{equation}
we assume that the shape is symmetric, and we neglect the disturbances.
\begin{boxdefinition}{Simplified Time Evolution of the Quadrotor}{}
    \begin{align}
& \ddot{x} = -(\cos(\psi) \sin(\vartheta) \cos(\varphi) + \sin(\psi) \sin(\varphi)) \frac{T}{m} \label{ddotx_quadrotor} \\
& \ddot{y} = -(\sin(\psi) \sin(\vartheta) \cos(\varphi) - \sin(\varphi) \cos(\psi)) \frac{T}{m} \label{ddoty_quadrotor} \\
& \ddot{z} = - \cos(\vartheta) \cos(\varphi) \frac{T}{m} + g \label{ddotz_quadrotor} \\
& \ddot{\varphi} = \frac{\tau_\varphi}{I_x} \\
& \ddot{\vartheta} = \frac{\tau_\vartheta}{I_y} \\
& \ddot{\psi} = \frac{\tau_\psi}{I_z}.
\end{align}
\end{boxdefinition}
\noindent Note how we changed the notation for the acceleration ($\ddot x=\dot v_x,\ \ddot y=\dot v_y,\ \ddot z=\dot v_z$).
\subsection{Linear Control}
Let's denote\begin{equation}
    \mathbf p=\begin{pmatrix}
        x\\y\\z 
    \end{pmatrix},\quad 
    \boldsymbol{\Theta}=\begin{pmatrix}
        \varphi\\\vartheta\\\psi
    \end{pmatrix},\quad \boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}
        \tau_\varphi\\\tau_\vartheta\\\tau_\psi 
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
we rewrite the state as\begin{equation}
     \boldsymbol{\xi}=\begin{pmatrix}
        \mathbf p^\top&\dot {\mathbf p}^\top& \boldsymbol{\Theta}^\top&\dot{ \boldsymbol{\Theta}}^\top
     \end{pmatrix}^\top
\end{equation}
with our input vector:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf u = \begin{pmatrix}
        T\\ \boldsymbol{\Gamma}
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Let's separate the translational and rotational dynamics:\begin{align}
    \ddot{\mathbf p}=&g\mathbf e_3-{}^iR_b(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\frac{T}{m}\mathbf e_3\label{traslational_dynamic}\\ 
\ddot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}=&J^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}. 
\end{align}
$\mathbf e_3=(0, \ 0, \ 1)^\top$ is the unit vector pointing downward, previously denoted $\mathbf z$.
Managing the nonlinear dynamics is hard for the real-time controller. We apply linearization around an equilibrium point, in particular in a hovering condition.
Let \begin{equation}
    \mathbf p_e=\begin{pmatrix}
        x_e\\y_e\\z_e
    \end{pmatrix},\quad 
    \boldsymbol{\Theta}_e=\begin{pmatrix}
        \varphi_e\\\vartheta_e\\\psi_e
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
be our equilibrium point.
We assume that our drone is perfectly horizontal, i.e., $\varphi_e=\vartheta_e=0$ with also $\psi_e=0$, and there are no active torques: $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\mathbf 0$. The thrust perfectly balances the gravity:\begin{equation}
    T_e=mg.
\end{equation}
The full \textit{hovering equilibrium} condition is expressed as follows:\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\Theta}_e=\mathbf 0,\quad 
    \boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\mathbf 0,\quad 
    T_e=mg,\quad 
    \dot{\mathbf p_e}=\mathbf 0,\quad 
    \dot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}_e=\mathbf 0.
\end{equation}
We denote $\ddot{\mathbf p}=\mathbf F(\bTheta,T)$ the translational part in Equation \eqref{traslational_dynamic}.
We approximate a small variation of $\ddot{\mathbf p}$ with its first order expansion around the equilibrium point:\begin{equation}\label{delta_ddp}
    \delta\ddot{\mathbf p}\simeq \frac{\partial\mathbf F}{\partial\bTheta}\Big|_e\delta\bTheta+\frac{\partial\mathbf F}{\partial T}\Big|_e\delta T
\end{equation}
with $\delta T$ and $\delta\bTheta$ being small variations. 
To compute the expansion, we need to compute the variation of the rotation matrix ${}^iR_b(\bTheta)$, given by the small variation $\delta\bTheta$ of the angles around $\bTheta_e=\mathbf 0$. We have\begin{equation}
    \delta\ {}^iR_b=\frac{\partial {}^iR_b}{\partial\varphi}\Big|_{\mathbf 0}\delta\varphi+
    \frac{\partial {}^iR_b}{\partial\vartheta}\Big|_{\mathbf 0}\delta\vartheta+
    \frac{\partial {}^iR_b}{\partial\psi}\Big|_{\mathbf 0}\delta\psi.
\end{equation}
We recall that ${}^iR_b(\bTheta)=R_z(\psi)R_y(\vartheta)R_x(\varphi)$, hence (without showing the whole calculation):\begin{equation}
    \delta {}^iR_b=\begin{pmatrix}
        0&-\delta\psi&\delta\vartheta\\ 
        \delta\psi&0&-\delta\varphi\\ 
        -\delta\vartheta&\delta\varphi&0 
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
this is exactly the skew-symmetric matrix $S(\delta\bTheta)$. Now we can make explicit the formula in Equation \eqref{delta_ddp}:\begin{align}
    \frac{\partial\mathbf F}{\partial\bTheta}\Big|_e\delta\bTheta&=-gS(\delta\bTheta)\mathbf e_3\\[1.5ex]
    \frac{\partial\mathbf F}{\partial T}\Big|_e\delta T&=-\frac{\delta T}{m}\mathbf e_3\\[1.5ex]
    \implies 
     \delta\ddot{\mathbf p}&\simeq -g\begin{pmatrix}
        \delta\vartheta\\-\delta\varphi\\0 
     \end{pmatrix}-\frac{\delta T}{m}\mathbf e_3.
\end{align}
Now we drop the notation with the $\delta$ (small variations), the quadrotor system linearized around an equilibrium point is governed by the following equations:\begin{align}
    &\ddot x=-g\vartheta,&\ddot\vartheta=\frac{\tau_\vartheta}{I_y},\\
    &\ddot y=g\varphi,&\ddot\varphi=\frac{\tau_\varphi}{I_x},\\
    &\ddot z=-\frac{(T-mg)}{m},&\ddot\psi=\frac{\tau_\psi}{I_z}.
\end{align} 
This system is linear and decoupled:\begin{itemize}
    \item In order to control $x$, we can control $\vartheta$ through $\tau_\vartheta$, hence $x$ is controllable with a chain of four integrators.
    \item In order to control $y$, we can control $\varphi$ through $\tau_\varphi$, hence $y$ is controllable with a chain of four integrators.
    \item In order to control $z$ we use $T$ in a chain of two integrators.
    \item In order to control $\psi$ we use $\tau_\psi$ in a chain of two integrators.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Basic Control and Planning Problems}\label{basic_control_planning}
The basic control objectives for a quadrotor are the following:\begin{itemize}
    \item attitude control,
    \item height control,
    \item position control,
    \item hovering-to-hovering motion,
    \item waypoint navigation, 
    \item trajectory planning and tracking,
    \item obstacle avoidance. 
\end{itemize}
With \textbf{attitude control}, we mean the process of determining the torques $\tau_\varphi,\tau_\vartheta,\tau_\psi$ needed to obtain a stable desired attitude $\varphi_d,\vartheta_d,\psi_d$. A simple solution is a proportional-derivative (PD) action on the error.
\begin{boxdefinition}{PD Controller for the Attitude}{PD_attitude}
\begin{align}
    &\tau_\varphi=K_{\varphi p}(\varphi_d-\varphi)+K_{\varphi d}(\dot \varphi_d-\dot \varphi)\\
    &\tau_\vartheta=K_{\vartheta p}(\vartheta_d-\vartheta)+K_{\vartheta d}(\dot \vartheta_d-\dot \vartheta)\\
    &\tau_\psi=K_{\psi p}(\psi_d-\psi)+K_{\psi d}(\dot \psi_d-\dot \psi).
\end{align}
\end{boxdefinition}
\noindent For the \textbf{height control} we consider the dynamical model, in particular Equation \eqref{ddotz_quadrotor}. We choose the value of $T$ that linearizes the system. 
\begin{boxdefinition}{Height Control with FBL}{}
    To bring the quadrotor to a desired height $z_d$ and keep it there, we apply feedback linearization (FBL) plus a PD controller.
    \begin{equation}
            T=\frac{m}{\cos\vartheta\cos\varphi}[g-\ddot z_d-K_{zp}(z_d-z)-K_{zd}(\dot z_d-\dot z)].
    \end{equation}
\end{boxdefinition}
\begin{boxobservation}{}{}
A quadrotor is an underactuated system: it has 6 degrees of freedom in space ($x, y, z$ and three attitude angles), but only 4 independent control inputs (the total thrust $T$ and the three torque moments $\tau_\varphi, \tau_\vartheta, \tau_\psi$). There are no side motors to push the drone directly along the $x$ axis or the $y$ axis. To translate horizontally, the drone must first tilt (rotate) so that a component of the thrust $T$ points in the desired direction.
\end{boxobservation}
Given the underactuated nature of the quadrotor, while we are controlling the altitude $z$ we cannot directly control $x$ and $y$, hence we adopt a \textit{hierarchical} control scheme.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item First, we assume that we have direct control on $x$ and $y$, with two \textbf{virtual control inputs} $u_x$ and $u_y$:\begin{align}
        &\ddot x=u_x\\ 
        &\ddot y=u_y.
    \end{align}
    \item These virtual control inputs are defined as a PD action on the error for stabilization:\begin{align}
        &u_x=\ddot x_d+K_{xp}(x_d-x)+K_{xd}(\dot x_d-\dot x)\\
        &u_y=\ddot y_d+K_{yp}(y_d-y)+K_{yd}(\dot y_d-\dot y).
    \end{align}
    \item From the dynamics in Equations \eqref{ddotx_quadrotor} and \eqref{ddoty_quadrotor}, we denote $T_x$ and $T_y$ the normalized components of the thrust along the inertial axes $x$ and $y$:\begin{align}
        &\ddot{x} = \underbrace{(-\cos\varphi \sin\vartheta \cos\psi - \sin\varphi \sin\psi)}_{T_x} \frac{T}{m}=T_x\frac{T}{m}\label{T_x}\\
        &\ddot{y} = \underbrace{(-\cos\varphi \sin\vartheta \sin\psi + \sin\varphi \cos\psi)}_{T_y} \frac{T}{m}=T_y\frac{T}{m}.\label{T_y}
    \end{align}
    \item Let's equate the real expressions to the virtual accelerations:\begin{align}
        &T_x \frac{T}{m} = u_x \implies T_x = \frac{m}{T} u_x = \frac{m}{T} \left[ K_{xp}(x_d - x) + K_{xd}(\dot{x}_d - \dot{x}) + \ddot{x}_d \right]\\
        &T_y \frac{T}{m} = u_y \implies T_y = \frac{m}{T} u_y = \frac{m}{T} \left[ K_{yp}(y_d - y) + K_{yd}(\dot{y}_d - \dot{y}) + \ddot{y}_d \right]
    \end{align}
    in this way we get the desired value for $T_x$ and $T_y$ in order to achieve $u_x$ and $u_y$.
    \item At this point we have to solve \eqref{T_x} and \eqref{T_y} for the roll and the pitch to get the desired values $\varphi_d$ and $\vartheta_d$:\begin{align}
        &\varphi_d=\arcsin\left(T_y\cos\psi-T_x\sin\psi\right)\\ 
        &\vartheta_d=-\arcsin\left(\frac{T_x\cos\psi+T_y\sin\psi}{\cos\varphi_d}\right).
    \end{align}
    \item Finally, the inner loop will generate the desired values for $(\tau_\varphi, \tau_\vartheta, \tau_\psi)$ to achieve $\varphi_d$ and $\vartheta_d$.
\end{enumerate}
To recap the whole control scheme:\begin{itemize}
    \item  The quadrotor has no laterally oriented propellers, and generates a single main thrust $T$ perpendicular to its frame. Consequently, there is no independent direct force on $x$ and $y$.
    \item $T$ is set as a priority to support the weight of the quadrotor and govern the $z$ altitude.
    \item  If we keep the drone perfectly horizontal, the thrust only points upwards. If, however, we tilt it in roll ($\varphi$) and pitch ($\vartheta$), the thrust vector is decomposed: one part continues to compensate for gravity along $z$, while the other is projected onto the horizontal plane.
    \item  The position loop calculates how much horizontal acceleration $(\ddot{x}, \ddot{y})$ is needed to reach the target and, working backwards, determines exactly which roll and pitch angles $(\varphi_d, \vartheta_d)$ project the necessary amount of thrust $T$ in that precise direction.
\end{itemize}

\section{Backstepping}
For a dynamical system:\begin{equation}
    \begin{cases}
        \dot{\mathbf x}=f(\mathbf x)\\ 
        f(\mathbf 0)=\mathbf 0
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
we recall that a Lyapunov function is a scalar function that depends on $\mathbf x$, is continuously differentiable and positive definite:\begin{align}
    &V(\mathbf x)>0,\quad \forall \mathbf x\ne\mathbf 0\\
    &V(\mathbf 0)=0.
\end{align}
If $\dot V=\frac{\partial V}{\partial \mathbf x}f(\mathbf x)$ is negative definite, then the equilibrium $\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$ is asymptotically stable. We also know that:\begin{itemize}
    \item If $V$ is radially unbounded, the system is globally asymptotically stable.
    \item If $\exists c_1>0,c_2>0,c_3>0$ with $c_1\le c_2$ such that \begin{align}
        &c_1\|\mathbf x\|^2\le V\le c_2\|\mathbf x\|^2,\\
        &\dot V \le -c_3\|\mathbf x\|^2
    \end{align}
    the system is globally exponentially stable.
    \item If $\dot V$ is negative semidefinite, the asymptotic stability can be proved using LaSalle's invariance principle.
\end{itemize}
In general, $V$ is hard to find, so the backstepping method is used to construct both $V$ and the control law; it renders an equilibrium point or a trajectory of a nonlinear system globally asymptotically stable (GAS).

The assumption is that our system is in strict-feedback (triangular) form; the quadrotor model satisfies this assumption. We will construct a simple two-dimensional example of how backstepping works. We have the following nonlinear system:\begin{align}
    &\dot x_1=f_1(x_1)+g_1(x_1)x_2\\ 
    &\dot x_2=f_2(x_1,x_2)+g_2(x_1,x_2)u
\end{align}
with $x_1=0$ being an equilibrium for the first subsystem. Notice the triangular structure: the first subsystem that describes the dynamics of $x_1$ depends nonlinearly only on $x_1$, and linearly on $x_2$. The second subsystem depends linearly on $u$ (control input) and nonlinearly on $(x_1,x_2)$. We also assume that
\begin{equation}
    \begin{matrix}
        g_1(x_1)\ne 0\\ 
        g_2(x_1,x_2)\ne 0
    \end{matrix}\quad\quad\forall(x_1,x_2).
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Step 1}: We stabilize $x_1$ by assuming that $x_2$ is a control input (can be chosen arbitrarily):\begin{equation}
        x_2=\alpha(x_1)=\frac{1}{g_1(x_1)}[-f_1(x_1)-k_1x_1]
    \end{equation}
    with the Lyapunov function $V_1=\frac{1}{2}x_1^2$. This leads to a simple differential equation:\begin{equation}
        \dot x_1=-k_1x_1
    \end{equation}
    and\begin{equation}
        \dot V_1=-k_1x_1^2
    \end{equation}
    hence the controller will globally stabilize $x_1$ to $0$.
    \item \textbf{Step 2}: In reality, $x_2$ has its own dynamics and is not necessarily equal to our \textit{virtual} control input $\alpha(x_1)$, hence we define an error:\begin{equation}
        z=x_2-\alpha(x_1)
    \end{equation}
    the dynamics of this error is\begin{equation}
        \dot z=\dot x_2-\dot \alpha
    \end{equation}
    we substitute $\dot x_2$ with its dynamics:\begin{equation}
         \dot z=f_2+g_2u-\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial x_1}\dot x_1
    \end{equation}
    notice how we dropped the dependencies (for the sake of notational simplicity). Now we substitute $\dot x_1$ with its dynamics:\begin{equation}\label{dot_z}
         \dot z=f_2+g_2u-\frac{\partial\alpha}{\partial x_1}(f_1+g_1x_2).
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
For the system in Equation \eqref{dot_z} we consider the following Lyapunov function: $$V=\frac{1}{2}x_1^2+\frac{1}{2}z^2$$ that is zero only at $x_1=0, z=0$. We want to stabilize $z$ to $0$ (nullify the error on $x_2$). The derivative is:
\begin{equation}
    \dot V=-k_1x_1^2+x_1g_1z+z(f_2+g_2u-\dot\alpha).
\end{equation}
To reach the desired behavior, we choose $u$ (our real control input) in order to make $\dot V$ negative definite:\begin{align}
    u=&\frac{1}{g_2}[-f_2+\dot\alpha-k_2z-x_1g_1]\implies \\
    \dot V = &-k_1x_1^2-k_2z^2\le -2\min(k_1,k_2)V
\end{align}
with $k_1,k_2$ being two positive real numbers. The term $-k_2z$ drives $x_2$ to $\alpha(x_1)$. This control law achieves exponential stability for the system in $x_1$ and $z$, and global asymptotic stability in $x_1$ and $x_2$. The achieved closed loop system in the error coordinates is\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \dot x_1\\\dot z 
    \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}
        -k_1&g_1\\ 
        -g_1&-k_2
    \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}
        x_1\\z
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The backstepping method can be recursively applied to a general triangular $n$-th order system:\begin{align}
    \dot x_i =& f_i(x_1,\dots, x_i)+g_i(x_1,\dots,x_i)x_{i+1},\quad \quad i=1,\dots,n-1\\ 
    \dot x_n=&f_n(\mathbf x)+g_n(\mathbf x)u.
\end{align}
For each step we define the error $z_i$, with the addition of a term to the Lyapunov function:\begin{align}
    &\dot z_i=-k_iz_i-g_{i-1}z_{i-1}+g_iz_{i+1}\\
    &\dot V =-\sum_ik_iz_i^2.
\end{align}
The backstepping method is a design procedure, not a defined control law, and it does not need to cancel the nonlinearities of a system to achieve stability. The problem is that the complexity of this procedure grows exponentially since each step needs the analytical derivative of the previous virtual input.
\subsection{Backstepping for Height Control}
Let's see an application of backstepping. The height dynamics of the quadrotor is the following:\begin{align}
    &\dot z = v_z\\ 
    &\dot v_z=g-\cos\varphi\cos\vartheta\frac{T}{m}
\end{align}
we choose $T$ in order to compensate for gravity and linearize the system:\begin{equation}
    T=\frac{m}{\cos\varphi\cos\vartheta}(g-\tilde T)\implies 
    \dot v_z=\tilde T
\end{equation}
with $\tilde T$ to be chosen later.
Given the desired height $z_d(t)$ we define the error:\begin{equation}
    e_z=z_d-z\implies \dot e_z=\dot z_d-v_z.
\end{equation}
If the velocity $v_z$ were the control input, we would choose:\begin{equation}\label{desired_vz}
    v_z=K_{zp}e_z+\dot z_d\implies \dot e_z=-K_{zp}e_z
\end{equation}
but $v_z$ has its own dynamics, hence we consider the formula in Equation \eqref{desired_vz} as a virtual control input denoted $v_z^d$:
\begin{equation}
    v^d_z=K_{zp}e_z+\dot z_d
\end{equation}
so we can define the error between the virtual input and the real velocity:\begin{equation}
     e_{v_z}=v_z^d-v_z
\end{equation}
furthermore:\begin{align}
    \dot e_z&=-K_{zp}e_z+e_{v_z}
    \\\dot e_{v_z}&=K_{zp}\dot e_z+\ddot z_d-\tilde T.
\end{align}
The Lyapunov function becomes:\begin{align}
    V&=\frac{1}{2}(e_z^2+e_{v_z}^2)
    \\\dot V&=-K_{zp}e_z^2+e_{v_z}(
        e_z+K_{zp}\dot e_z+\ddot z_d-\tilde T
    )
\end{align}
now we choose $\tilde T$ to make $\dot V$ negative definite\begin{align}
    \tilde T  &=\ddot z_d+e_z+K_{zp}\dot e_z+K_{zd}e_{v_z}\implies \\ 
    \dot V &= -K_{zp}e_z^2-K_{zd}e_{v_z}^2
\end{align}
by putting everything together, the final control law is:\begin{equation}
    T = \frac{m}{\cos \varphi \cos \vartheta} \left( g - \ddot{z}_d - (1 + K_{zp} K_{zd}) e_z - (K_{zp} + K_{zd}) \dot{e}_z \right).
\end{equation}
\subsection{PD Compared With Backstepping}
What are the differences between the PD seen in Section \ref{basic_control_planning} and the backstepping? They share a similar structure, but with a different choice of coordinates. The control laws are identical if we set the gain of the PD as follows:\begin{align}
    K_p&=1+K_{zp}K_{zd}\\
    K_d&=K_{zp}+K_{zd}.
\end{align}
The PD controller aims to nullify the position and velocity errors $(e_z,\dot e_z)$, while the backstepping law considers $e_{v_z}=\dot e_z+\greenText{K_{zp}e_z}$ instead of $\dot e_z$, hence the desired velocity already contains a term related to the \greenText{ position correction}.

The gains have different roles:\begin{itemize}
    \item $K_{zp}$ controls the decay rate of the position error, when the velocity error is zero.
    \item $K_{zd}$ is the decay rate of the velocity error (the inner loop of the backstepping).
    \item In the PD controller, the natural frequency and the damping ratio of the error dynamics are given by\begin{align}
        \omega_n&=\sqrt{K_p}\\
        \zeta&=\frac{K_d}{2\sqrt{K_p}}.
    \end{align}
\end{itemize}
With the same choices for the gains $K_p=K_{zp},K_d=K_{zd}$ the closed-loop poles are (by setting $K_p=K_d=2$):\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{backstepping}: $-2\pm i$ 
    \item \textbf{PD}: $-1\pm i$ (twice as slow).
\end{itemize}
A PD controller can be obtained from the backstepping procedure. The backstepping provides a hierarchical tuning procedure, with an explicit Lyapunov function.

\subsection{Integral Backstepping}
Now we consider the roll dynamics:\begin{equation}
    \ddot\varphi=\frac{\tau_\varphi}{I_x}+h_\varphi
\end{equation}
$h_\varphi$ represents the nonlinear terms, for example the gyroscopic effects.
We define the integral action of the error:\begin{align}
    &e_1=\varphi_d-\varphi,\\
    &\chi =\int_0^t e_1ds
\end{align}
then we define the desired (or virtual) roll velocity as:\begin{equation}
    \omega_d=c_1e_1+\dot \varphi_d+\lambda\chi
\end{equation}
with $c_1$ and $\lambda$ being control gains. We define a second error on the desired velocity:\begin{equation}
    e_2=\omega_d-\dot\varphi
\end{equation}
by deriving with respect to time the error equations we get:\begin{align}
    \dot e_1&=-c_1e_1-\lambda\chi+e_2       \\ 
    \dot e_2&=c_1\dot e_1+\ddot\varphi_d+\lambda e_1-\ddot\varphi
\end{align}
to guarantee the asymptotic stability of the system we want to impose the following behavior for the error:\begin{equation}
    \dot e_2 = -c_2 e_2 - e_1
\end{equation}
hence we choose the following control law:\begin{equation}
    \tau_\varphi = I_x \left[ (1 - c_1^2 + \lambda)e_1 + (c_1 + c_2)e_2 - c_1 \lambda \chi + \ddot{\varphi}_d - h_\varphi\right]
\end{equation}
the same construction can be done for the altitude and the position. The stability of the system with this control law can be proved by defining the following Lyapunov candidate:\begin{align}
    V&=\frac{1}{2}\lambda\chi^2+\frac{1}{2}e_1^2+\frac{1}{2}e_2^2\\ 
    \dot V&=-c_1e_1^2-c_2e_2^2
\end{align}
on the equilibrium $e_1=e_2=0$ the system becomes:\begin{equation}
    \dot e_1=-\lambda\chi 
\end{equation}
that is zero when $\chi=0$ (GAS proved by LaSalle).

\section{Dynamic Feedback Linearization}
One of the most important and effective ways to control a nonlinear dynamical system is to make it linear through feedback.
\begin{boxdefinition}{Feedback Linearization}{}
    Feedback linearization is the exact cancellation of the nonlinearities by
    feedback and change of coordinates.
\end{boxdefinition}
\noindent\textbf{Recap of the model}: For control purposes, we are using the (already seen) simplified model that assumes small changes for $\varphi$ and $\vartheta$
$$(\dot\varphi,\dot\vartheta,\dot\psi)\simeq(p,q,r)$$
and negligible aerodynamics/gyroscopic effects:\begin{align}
    &\ddot x = -(\cos\psi\sin\vartheta\cos\varphi+\sin\psi\sin\varphi)\frac{T}{m}\\
    &\ddot y = -(\sin\psi\sin\vartheta\cos\varphi-\sin\varphi\cos\psi)\frac{T}{m}\\
    &\ddot z = -\cos\vartheta\cos\varphi\frac{T}{m}+g\\
    & \ddot{\varphi} = \frac{\tau_\varphi}{I_x} \\
    & \ddot{\vartheta} = \frac{\tau_\vartheta}{I_y} \\
    & \ddot{\psi} = \frac{\tau_\psi}{I_z}\label{simplified_yaw_dynamics}.
\end{align}
We have used the symbol $\bxi$ for the state and $\mathbf u$ for the inputs. The compact form is\begin{equation}\label{compact_form}
\begin{cases}
    \ddot{\mathbf p}=g\mathbf e_3-\frac{T}{m}\mathbf b(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\\ 
    \ddot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}=J^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where we defined:\begin{align}
   &\mathbf  b(\boldsymbol{\Theta})={}^iR_b(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\mathbf e_3\\ 
   & \mathbf p = \begin{pmatrix}
    x&y&z
   \end{pmatrix}^\top\\ 
   &\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\begin{pmatrix}
   \varphi&\vartheta&\psi
   \end{pmatrix}^\top\\
   &\boldsymbol{\Gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}
   \tau_\varphi&\tau_\vartheta&\tau_\psi
   \end{pmatrix}^\top\\
   &J=\text{diag}(I_x,I_y,I_z).
\end{align}
\subsection{Static Linearization}
Before discussing the linearization of the quadrotor model, we will give some definitions for a general nonlinear system, but keep in mind the model in Equation \eqref{compact_form}, as we will need it later.\bigskip 

\noindent Consider the following nonlinear system:\begin{equation}
    \dot\bxi=f(\bxi)+g(\bxi)\mathbf u\quad\quad\begin{matrix}\bxi\in\R^n\\\mathbf u \in \R^m\end{matrix}.
\end{equation}
If possible, we \textit{want to find} a \textbf{static} state feedback and a change of coordinates:\begin{align}
    \mathbf u&=\alpha(\bxi)+\beta(\bxi)\mathbf v\\ \mathbf z&=\phi(\bxi)
\end{align}
with $\beta$ a non-singular function and $\phi$ a diffeomorphism, such that the closed-loop system becomes linear and controllable:\begin{align}
    &\dot\bxi=f(\bxi)+g(\bxi)\greenText{\mathbf u}\implies \\
    &\dot\bxi=f(\bxi)+g(\bxi)\greenText{(\alpha(\bxi)+\beta(\bxi)\mathbf v)} \end{align}
    since $\bxi=\phi^{-1}(\mathbf z)$:
    \begin{align}
    &\frac{d}{dt}\phi^{-1}(\mathbf z)=f(\phi^{-1}(\mathbf z))+g(\phi^{-1}(\mathbf z))(\alpha(\phi^{-1}(\mathbf z))+\beta({\phi^{-1}(\mathbf z)})\mathbf v)\implies \\ 
    &\dot {\mathbf z}=A\mathbf z+B\mathbf v 
\end{align} 
with $A$ and $B$ being constant matrices. This linearization is exact and holds whenever $\beta$ and $\phi$ are well defined; the control design is completed by choosing $\mathbf v$ through the rules of linear control theory, and since $\phi$ is a diffeomorphism, by controlling $\mathbf z$ we can indirectly control $\bxi$.
\subsection{Input-Output Linearization}
With this method, we focus on a subset of the state vector that we want to control, by allowing (in general) a residual internal dynamics on the remaining variables. We choose $m$ variables (same number of inputs) with a (sufficiently smooth) function $h$:\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\eta}=h(\bxi)\in\R^m 
\end{equation}
We know that the inputs $\mathbf u$ are present in $\dot\bxi$, hence by differentiating $h(\bxi)$, at some point one or more inputs of $\mathbf u$ will appear. $\boldsymbol{\eta}$ is a vector of functions $\eta_i$, $1\le i \le m$, we have to differentiate each of these functions until \textit{at least} one of the inputs of $\mathbf u$ appears.
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Input-Output Linearization}\label{alg:input_output_lin}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\For{$i=1,\dots,m$}
\State $found \gets \text{false}$
\For{$r_i=1,\dots,+\infty$}
\For{$j=1,\dots,m$}
\If{$u_j$ is present in $\eta_i^{(r_i)}$}
\State save $\eta_i^{(r_i)}$
\State $found \gets \text{true}$
\State break
\EndIf
\EndFor
\If{$found$}
\State break
\EndIf
\EndFor
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\noindent The explicit expression of $\eta_i^{(r_i)}$ is\begin{equation}\label{eta_i}
    \eta_i^{(r_i)}=\underbrace{L_f^{r_i}h_i(\bxi)}_{\text{Lie derivative}}+\sum_{j=1}^mL_{g_j}L_f^{r_i-1}h_i(\bxi)u_j.
\end{equation}
where $r_i$ is the relative degree of differentiation for the $i$-th component of $\boldsymbol{\eta}$. Clearly, $h$ should be in the class $C^r$ with $r=\max_ir_i$. The Lie derivatives are defined as follows:\begin{align}
    &L_f^0h_i(\bxi)=h_i(\bxi)\\[1.5ex]
    &L_f^1h_i(\bxi)=L_fh_i(\bxi) = \frac{\partial h_i(\bxi)}{\partial \bxi}f(\bxi)\\[1.5ex]
    &L_f^{k}h_i(\bxi) = \frac{\partial (L_f^{k-1}h_i)}{\partial \bxi}f(\bxi).
\end{align}
$L_{g_j} L_f^{r_i - 1} h_i(\bxi)$ represents the Lie derivative of the intermediate scalar function $L_f^{r_i - 1} h_i(\bxi)$ along the input vector field $g_j(\bxi)$:\begin{equation}
    L_{g_j} L_f^{r_i - 1} h_i(\bxi) = \frac{\partial (L_f^{r_i - 1} h_i(\bxi))}{\partial \bxi} g_j(\bxi).
\end{equation}
\begin{boxnotation}{}{}
When the superscript of a symbol is enclosed in round brackets, it denotes a derivative; if there are no brackets, it is an exponent or an index:\begin{align}
    &a^k \text{ is $a$ raised to the power $k$}\\
    &a^{(k)} \text{ is the $k$-th derivative of the function $a$.}
\end{align}
\end{boxnotation}
For the particular case of the Lie derivatives, the superscript $k$ in $L_f^k$ does not represent an exponent but an order of differentiation, even though it is not enclosed in brackets.\bigskip

\noindent We can stack the $m$ equations defined in \eqref{eta_i} and write the system in compact form:\begin{equation}\label{i_o_linearized_model}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \eta_1^{(r_1)}\\\vdots\\
        \eta_m^{(r_m)}
    \end{pmatrix}=I(\bxi)+D(\bxi)\mathbf u
\end{equation}
$D(\bxi)$ is called the \textbf{decoupling matrix}. If it is non-singular, then, for $\mathbf v\in\R^m$, choosing the following control law:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf u=D^{-1}(\bxi)(-I(\bxi)+{\mathbf v})
\end{equation}
we achieve the following dynamic behavior:\begin{equation}
    \eta_i^{(r_i)}=v_i,\quad i=1,\dots,m 
\end{equation}
we obtain $m$ \textbf{decoupled chains of integrators} from $v_i$ to $\eta_i$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item if $\sum_ir_i=n$, we have \textbf{full state linearization}, and $\mathbf z=(\eta_i,\eta_i^{(1)},\dots,\eta_i^{(r_i-1)})$, for $i=1,\dots,m$, are coordinates.
    \item if $\sum_ir_i<n$, there will be an $(n-\sum_ir_i)$-dimensional internal dynamics, whose stability must be checked separately.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Linearization of the Quadrotor Model}
From the quadrotor model in Equation \eqref{compact_form} we consider as natural outputs the position and the yaw angle:\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\eta}=\begin{pmatrix}
        x&y&z&\psi
    \end{pmatrix}^\top 
\end{equation}
the model relative only to these variables is:\begin{equation}\label{compact_model_2}
    \ddot{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=\begin{pmatrix}
        \ddot{\mathbf p}\\ \ddot\psi 
    \end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}
        g\mathbf e_3\\ 0
    \end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}
        -\frac{1}{m}\mathbf b(\boldsymbol{\Theta})&\mathbf 0&\mathbf 0&\mathbf 0\\ 
        0&0&0&\frac{1}{I_z}
    \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}
        T\\ \tau_\varphi\\\tau_\vartheta\\\tau_\psi 
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The quadrotor has six degrees of freedom and four control inputs; its underactuated nature makes it impossible to linearize the full state with a simple static feedback linearization $\mathbf u=\alpha(\bxi)+\beta(\bxi)\mathbf v$. If we tried to linearize only the output statically, we would be left with non-linearized internal dynamics.

To overcome this problem, we must add a \textbf{dynamic compensator}, by introducing a set of $\nu$ additional states to the controller:\begin{equation}
    \bzeta\in\R^\nu \text{ (additional states)}
\end{equation}
we have to specify a dynamics for these states:\begin{equation}
    \dot\bzeta=a(\bxi,\bzeta)+b(\bxi,\bzeta)\mathbf u 
\end{equation}
defined by the functions $a,b$ such that, together with the controller:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf u=c(\bxi,\bzeta)+d(\bxi,\bzeta)\mathbf v 
\end{equation}
for some functions $c,d$ the closed-loop system, of dimension $n+\nu$, is
equivalent under a state transformation $\mathbf z=\phi(\bxi,\bzeta)$ to a \textit{linear
controllable system}. Physically, in the quadrotor, the auxiliary state $\bzeta$ typically consists of integrators added on the thrust input $T$. By integrating the thrust  twice, the true thrust becomes a state coordinate of the extended system, delaying the immediate appearance of the input into the derivatives and allowing the rotational dynamics to align with the translational ones without singularities. \bigskip

Let's see the \textbf{algorithm of dynamic extension}: the construction procedure used to find the dynamic compensator and completely linearize the system.\begin{enumerate}
    \item We choose an $m$-dimensional output $\boldsymbol{\eta}=h(\bxi)$.
    \item We differentiate the outputs with respect to time until the inputs appear such that the decoupling matrix is non-singular (invertible).
    \item When differentiating the components of $\boldsymbol{\eta}$, it often happens that some inputs appear at a lower differentiation order than others, making the decoupling matrix singular. We add integrators on those input channels: the inputs become states of the compensator, their derivatives become the new inputs.
    \item If, at the end of the algorithm, the sum of the differentiation orders reached for each output equals the size of the extended state space:$$\sum_{i=1}^m r_i = n + \nu$$
    (where $n$ is the size of the original physical state and $\nu$ is the total number of integrators added), \textit{full input-state-output linearization} is obtained.
    \item Applying the inversion law on the extended system, the behavior between the new inputs $v_i$ and the physical outputs $\eta_i$ is exactly equivalent to $m$ independent and decoupled chains of pure integrators:\begin{equation}
        \eta_i^{(r_i)} = v_i, \quad i = 1, \dots, m.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
The linearizing quadrotor output that we choose is the position together with the yaw angle:\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\eta}=\begin{pmatrix}
        x&y&z&\psi 
    \end{pmatrix}^\top
\end{equation}
The model is the one in Equation \eqref{compact_model_2}. The inputs appear in a non-singular way only after differentiating $\eta_1,\eta_2,\eta_3$ four times and $\eta_4$ twice. The explicit expression of the vector $\mathbf b$ in the model is\begin{equation}
    \mathbf  b(\boldsymbol{\Theta})={}^iR_b(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\mathbf e_3
    =\begin{pmatrix}
    \cos\psi \sin\vartheta \cos\varphi + \sin\psi \sin\varphi \\
    \sin\psi \sin\vartheta \cos\varphi - \cos\psi \sin\varphi \\
    \cos\vartheta \cos\varphi
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
we will need its Jacobian, denoted $B$:\begin{equation}
    B=\frac{\partial\mathbf b}{\partial\boldsymbol{\Theta}}=\begin{pmatrix}
\sin\psi \cos\varphi - \cos\psi \sin\vartheta \sin\varphi & \cos\psi \cos\vartheta \cos\varphi & \cos\psi \sin\varphi - \sin\psi \sin\vartheta \cos\varphi \\
-\cos\psi \cos\varphi - \sin\psi \sin\vartheta \sin\varphi & \sin\psi \cos\vartheta \cos\varphi & \sin\psi \sin\varphi + \cos\psi \sin\vartheta \cos\varphi \\
-\cos\vartheta \sin\varphi & -\sin\vartheta \cos\varphi & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
By differentiating $\eta_1,\eta_2,\eta_3$ twice we get the translational part of the model:\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \ddot\eta_1&\ddot\eta_2&\ddot\eta_3
    \end{pmatrix}^\top=\ddot{\mathbf p}=g\mathbf e_3-\frac{T}{m}\mathbf b(\boldsymbol{\Theta})
\end{equation}
we keep differentiating until the inputs $T,\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$ appear:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf p^{(3)}=-\frac{1}{m}(\dot T\mathbf b+TB\dot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}})
\end{equation}
one last differentiation:\begin{equation}
     \mathbf p^{(4)}=-\frac{1}{m}(\ddot T\mathbf b+2\dot T B \dot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}+T\dot B \dot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}+TB\greenText{\ddot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}})
\end{equation}
$\ddot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}$ is directly mapped to $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$ through the inertia matrix:\begin{equation}
     \mathbf p^{(4)}=-\frac{1}{m}(\ddot T\mathbf b+2\dot T B \dot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}+T\dot B \dot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}+TBJ^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Gamma})
\end{equation}
finally, the inputs $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$ appear in the Cartesian snap $\mathbf p^{(4)}$. Since $\eta_4=\psi$, we need just two differentiations in order to get a non-singular dependence on $\tau_\psi$:\begin{equation}
    \ddot\psi=\frac{\tau_\psi}{I_z}.
\end{equation}
In order to control $\mathbf p^{(4)}$ we have to choose $\ddot T$, hence this will be our input, while $T$ and $\dot T$ become states:\begin{equation}
    \zeta_1=T,\quad\zeta_2=\dot T  
\end{equation}
the model becomes:\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix}
\mathbf p^{(4)} \\
\psi^{(2)}
\end{pmatrix}
=
\underbrace{
\begin{pmatrix}
-\dfrac{1}{m}\left( 2\zeta_2\,B\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}} + \zeta_1\,\dot{B}\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\Theta}} \right) \\
0
\end{pmatrix}
}_{I(\boldsymbol{\xi}, \boldsymbol{\zeta})}
+
\underbrace{
\begin{pmatrix}
-\dfrac{1}{m}\,\mathbf b & -\dfrac{\zeta_1}{m}\,B\,J^{-1} \\
0 & 0\quad 0\quad \dfrac{1}{I_z}
\end{pmatrix}
}_{D(\boldsymbol{\xi}, \boldsymbol{\zeta})}
\begin{pmatrix}
\ddot{T} \\
\tau_\varphi \\
\tau_\vartheta \\
\tau_\psi
\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
In order to have the inputs appear in a non-singular way, $D$ must be non-singular. Its determinant is\begin{equation}
    \det D=-\frac{T^2\cos\varphi}{m^3I_xI_yI_z}
\end{equation}
hence $D$ is non-singular if and only if $T\ne0$ ($T=0$ corresponds to free fall) and $\varphi\ne \pm\frac{\pi}{2}$. Away from the singularities, the sum of the relative degrees is\begin{equation}
    \sum_ir_i = 4+4+4+2=14=n+\nu  
\end{equation}
hence \textit{full state linearization} with no zero dynamics is achieved. 
The new state $\mathbf z$ that we choose through a diffeomorphism $\phi$ is given by the outputs and their derivatives:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf z=\phi(\bxi,\bzeta)=\begin{pmatrix}
        \mathbf p&
        \dot{\mathbf p}&
        \ddot{\mathbf p}&
        \dddot{\mathbf p}&
        \psi&\dot\psi
    \end{pmatrix}^\top\in\R^{14}
\end{equation}
we can act on $\mathbf p^{(4)}$ hence its derivatives of lower orders become part of the state, with:\begin{align}
    \ddot{\mathbf p}&=g\mathbf e_3-\frac{\zeta_1}{m}\mathbf b(\bTheta)\\
     \dddot{\mathbf p}&=-\frac{1}{m}(\zeta_2\mathbf b(\bTheta)+\zeta_1B(\bTheta)\dot\bTheta).
\end{align}
The linearizing and decoupling control law is\begin{equation}
    {\mathbf u}=D^{-1}(\bxi,\bzeta)(-I(\bxi,\bzeta)+\mathbf v).
\end{equation}
the closed loop system becomes:\begin{align}
    x^{(4)}&=v_1\\
    y^{(4)}&=v_2\\
    z^{(4)}&=v_3\\
    \ddot\psi&=v_4.
\end{align}
The system is linear in the coordinates $\mathbf z$.
To track a trajectory $\boldsymbol{\eta}_d$ we define the error $e_i=\eta_{id}-\eta_i$ for each $i$ and set a PD (with higher order derivatives):
\begin{align}
    &v_i = \eta^{(4)}_{id}+\sum_{j=1}^{4}c_{i,j-1}e_i^{(j-1)}\quad\quad i=1,2,3\\
    & v_4=\ddot\eta_{4d}+c_{4,0}e_4+c_{4,1}\dot e_4.
\end{align}
The system is stable and the tracking error, with an appropriate choice of the gains $c_{i,j}$, converges exponentially. The reference $\boldsymbol{\eta}_d$ must be differentiable four times at least for the first three components (position) and at least two times for the yaw $\psi$. The dynamic compensator has the following time evolution:\begin{align}
    &\dot\zeta_1=\zeta_2\\ 
    &\dot\zeta_2=D_1^{-1}(\bxi,\bzeta)(-I(\bxi,\bzeta)+\mathbf v)\\ 
    &T=\zeta_1\\ 
    &\tau_\varphi=D_2^{-1}(\bxi,\bzeta)(-I(\bxi,\bzeta)+\mathbf v)\\ 
     &\tau_\vartheta=D_3^{-1}(\bxi,\bzeta)(-I(\bxi,\bzeta)+\mathbf v)\\ 
      &\tau_\psi=D_4^{-1}(\bxi,\bzeta)(-I(\bxi,\bzeta)+\mathbf v)
\end{align}
with $D_i^{-1}$ being the $i$-th row of $D^{-1}$, hence $D_i^{-1}(\bxi,\bzeta)(-I(\bxi,\bzeta)+\mathbf v)$ is the scalar result of a product between row and column.

    \caption{Block diagram of the control system.}
\end{figure}
\subsection{Considering the Complete Model}
In this section we discuss the dynamic feedback linearization for the quadrotor model, by removing the assumption of small angles. We neglect only the aerodynamics and gyroscopic effects:
\begin{align}\label{full_model}
& \dot{x} = v_x & &\dot{\varphi} = p + s_\varphi \tan\vartheta q + c_\varphi \tan\vartheta r \\
& \dot{y} = v_y & &\dot{\vartheta} = c_\varphi q - s_\varphi r \\
& \dot{z} = v_z & &\dot{\psi} = s_\varphi \sec\vartheta q + c_\varphi \sec\vartheta r \\
& \dot{v}_x =  - (c_\psi s_\vartheta c_\varphi + s_\psi s_\varphi) \frac{T}{m} & & 
\dot{p} =  \frac{I_y - I_z}{I_x} q r + \frac{\tau_\varphi}{I_x}\label{dot_p} \\
& \dot{v}_y =  - (s_\psi s_\vartheta c_\varphi - s_\varphi c_\psi) \frac{T}{m} & & \dot{q} =    \frac{I_z - I_x}{I_y} p r + \frac{\tau_\vartheta}{I_y}\label{dot_q} \\
& \dot{v}_z =  g - c_\vartheta c_\varphi\frac{T}{m} 
& & \dot{r} = \frac{I_x - I_y}{I_z} p q + \frac{\tau_\psi}{I_z}
\end{align}
we are using the short notation for $\sin$ and $\cos$. We denote $\Bomega=(p,q,r)^\top$ the angular velocity, the model in compact form can be rewritten as:\begin{align}
    &\ddot{\mathbf p}=g\mathbf e_3-\frac{T}{m}R\mathbf e_3\\ 
    &\dot R=RS(\Bomega)\\ 
    &J\dot{\Bomega}=-\Bomega\times J\Bomega+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}.
\end{align}
We compute the dynamic feedback linearization (DFL) by setting the same output and extension:\begin{align}
    \boldsymbol{\eta}=\begin{pmatrix}
        x&y&z&\psi
    \end{pmatrix}^\top\\ 
    \bzeta=\begin{pmatrix}
        T&\dot T 
    \end{pmatrix}^\top
\end{align}
let's write out $\mathbf p^{(4)}$:\begin{align}
    \mathbf p^{(3)}&=\frac{d}{dt}\left(g\mathbf e_3-\frac{T}{m}R\mathbf e_3\right)\\
    &=-\frac{T}{m}\frac{d}{dt}(R\mathbf e_3)-\frac{\dot T}{m}R\mathbf e_3\\
    &=-\frac{1}{m}R(\dot T\mathbf e_3+T\Bomega\times\mathbf e_3)\implies \\ 
    \mathbf p^{(4)}&=-\frac{1}{m}R\begin{pmatrix}
        T\dot q+2\dot T q+ Tpr\\ 
        -T\dot p-2\dot Tp+Tqr\\ 
        \ddot T-T(p^2+q^2)
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{align}
the snap $\mathbf p^{(4)}$ depends on $\dot p,\dot q,\ddot T$. We substitute $\dot q,\dot p$ as in Equations \eqref{dot_p} and \eqref{dot_q}:\begin{equation}\label{snap_not_organized}
    \mathbf p^{(4)}=-\frac{1}{m}R\begin{pmatrix}
        T(\frac{I_z - I_x}{I_y} p r + \frac{\tau_\vartheta}{I_y})+2\dot T q+ Tpr\\ 
        -T( \frac{I_y - I_z}{I_x} q r + \frac{\tau_\varphi}{I_x})-2\dot Tp+Tqr\\ 
        \ddot T-T(p^2+q^2)
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
In order to reorganize the snap equation, we define the following matrix and vector:\begin{equation}
    N=\begin{pmatrix}
        0&0&\frac{T}{I_y}\\
        0&-\frac{T}{I_x}&0\\
        1&0&0
    \end{pmatrix},\quad 
    \mathbf n=\begin{pmatrix}
        (1+\frac{I_z-I_x}{I_y})Tpr+2\dot Tq\\ 
        (1-\frac{I_y-I_z}{I_x})Tqr-2\dot Tp\\ 
        -T(p^2+q^2)
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{snap_not_organized} becomes:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf p^{(4)}=
    \underbrace{-\frac{1}{m}R\mathbf n}_{I_p(\bxi,\bzeta)}\quad + \quad
    \underbrace{-\frac{1}{m}RN}_{D_p(\bxi,\bzeta)}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \ddot T\\\tau_\varphi\\\tau_\vartheta
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
$I_p,D_p$ are the translational part of the matrices $I,D$ of the model in the form in Equation \eqref{i_o_linearized_model}. Notice how the snap $\mathbf p^{(4)}$ does not depend on $\tau_\psi$. In the simplified model the yaw dynamics $\ddot\psi$ was a simple double integrator, as seen in Equation \eqref{simplified_yaw_dynamics}. This does not hold anymore, since:\begin{equation}
    \dot{\psi} = s_\varphi \sec\vartheta q + c_\varphi \sec\vartheta r
\end{equation}
before deriving $\ddot\psi$ we introduce the following scalar:\begin{equation}
   I_{\psi} = \frac{s_{\varphi} \frac{I_z-I_x}{I_y} p r + c_{\varphi} \frac{I_x-I_y}{I_z} p q}{c_{\vartheta}} + \frac{(c_{\varphi} q - s_{\varphi} r)\dot{\varphi}}{c_{\vartheta}} + \frac{(s_{\varphi} q + c_{\varphi} r) \tan \vartheta \, \dot{\vartheta}}{c_{\vartheta}}
\end{equation}
the yaw acceleration is exactly:\begin{equation}
    \ddot\psi=I_\psi+\frac{s_\varphi}{c_\vartheta I_y}\tau_\vartheta+\frac{c_\varphi}{c_\vartheta I_z}\tau_\psi
\end{equation}
now both the control torques $\tau_\vartheta,\tau_\psi$ act on $\ddot\psi$. The complete model is:\begin{equation}
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \mathbf p^{(4)}\\
        \ddot\psi 
    \end{pmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}
        -\frac{1}{m}R\mathbf n\\ I_\psi 
    \end{pmatrix}}_{I(\bxi,\bzeta)}+\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}
        &&& 0\\
        &-\frac{1}{m}RN&& 0\\ &&& 0  \\
        0&0&\frac{s_\varphi}{c_\vartheta I_y}&\frac{c_\varphi}{c_\vartheta I_z}
    \end{pmatrix}}_{D(\bxi,\bzeta)}\begin{pmatrix}
            \ddot T\\ \tau_\varphi\\\tau_\vartheta\\\tau_\psi
        \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
with\begin{equation}
    \det D=-\frac{T^2\cos\varphi}{m^3I_xI_yI_z\cos\vartheta}
\end{equation}
hence the model has a singularity on $T=0$ and $\varphi=\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$. Now we want to linearize this model, our virtual input is:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf v=\begin{pmatrix}
        v_1&v_2&v_3&v_4 
    \end{pmatrix}^\top
\end{equation}
with $v_1,v_2,v_3$ being relative to the snap and $v_4$ to the yaw. In order to linearize the snap we define the following vector:\begin{equation}
        \bmu=\begin{pmatrix}
            \mu_1\\\mu_2\\\mu_3 
        \end{pmatrix}=-mR^\top\begin{pmatrix}
        v_1\\ v_2\\ v_3
    \end{pmatrix}-\mathbf n
    \end{equation}
The linearizing control law is\begin{align}
    &\ddot T=\mu_3\\ 
    &\tau_\varphi=-\frac{I_x}{T}\mu_2\\
    &\tau_\vartheta=\frac{I_y}{T}\mu_1\\ 
    &\tau_\psi=\frac{I_zc_\vartheta}{c_\varphi}(v_4-I_\psi)-\frac{I_z}{I_y}\tan\varphi\tau_\vartheta
\end{align}
the system becomes:\begin{align}
        x^{(4)}&=v_1\\  y^{(4)}&=v_2\\  z^{(4)}&=v_3\\
        \ddot\psi &=v_4.
\end{align}

\subsection{Flatness}
\begin{boxdefinition}{Differentially Independent}{}
    Let $\mathbf y$ be a vector of quantities that changes over time. The components of  $\mathbf y$ are \textbf{differentially independent} if there is no differential equation that links the individual components of $\mathbf y$ together, i.e. it is not possible to find an equation of the form:\begin{equation}
        \Phi(\mathbf y,\dy,\ddy,\dots)=\mathbf 0.
    \end{equation}
\end{boxdefinition}
\begin{boxdefinition}{Differentially Flat System}{}
    A system $\dot\bxi=f(\bxi,\mathbf u)$ with $\mathbf u\in\R^m$ is \textbf{differentially flat} if there exist $m$ outputs:\begin{equation}
        \mathbf y = h(\bxi,\mathbf u,\dot{\mathbf u},\dots,\mathbf u^{(k)})
    \end{equation}
    called \textbf{flat outputs} such that the state $\bxi$ and the inputs $\mathbf u$ can be rewritten as functions of $\mathbf y$ and its derivatives (up to a finite order):\begin{align}
        \bxi&= \Psi_\xi(\mathbf y,\dot{\mathbf y},\dots, \mathbf y^{(r_\xi)})\\
        \mathbf u&=\Psi_u(\mathbf y,\dot{\mathbf y},\dots, \mathbf y^{(r_u)})
    \end{align}
    and the components of $\mathbf y$ are differentially independent.
\end{boxdefinition}
If a system is differentially flat, the trajectories of the system are in one-to-one correspondence
with arbitrary smooth curves $\mathbf y(t)$: there are no differential equations to integrate.
\begin{boxobservation}{}{}
    There does not exist a general test to check if a system is differentially flat, flat outputs are found by inspection, often
    with a physical meaning.
\end{boxobservation}
\noindent The quadrotor model is one of the best-known examples of a system with flat outputs, which are exactly:\begin{equation}
    \y=\begin{pmatrix}
        x&y&z&\psi
    \end{pmatrix}^\top
\end{equation}
by considering the inputs\begin{equation}
    \mathbf u=\begin{pmatrix}
        T&\tau_\varphi&\tau_\vartheta&\tau_\psi
    \end{pmatrix}^\top
\end{equation}
we want to derive the map $\Psi$ such that:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf u = \Psi(\mathbf y,\dot{\mathbf y},\dots, \mathbf y^{(r)})
\end{equation}
first, we express $T$ in function of $\ddot{\mathbf p}$, by the dynamic model:\begin{align}
    \ddot{\mathbf p}&=g\mathbf e_3-\frac{T}{m}R\mathbf e_3\implies \\
    T&=m\|g\mathbf e_3-\ddot{\mathbf p}\|
\end{align}
the versor of the thrust direction is:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf b = \frac{g\mathbf e_3-\ddot{\mathbf p}}{\|g\mathbf e_3-\ddot{\mathbf p}\|}
\end{equation}
from the jerk equation we can define a new vector:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf w = -mR^\top\mathbf p^{(3)}
\end{equation}
and get the following identities:\begin{align}
    &q=\frac{w_1}{T}\\ 
    &p=-\frac{w_2}{T}\\ 
    &\dot T = w_3
\end{align}
and\begin{equation}
    r=\frac{c_\vartheta\dot\psi-s_\varphi q}{c_\varphi}
\end{equation}
from the snap equation, we define:\begin{equation}
    \bmu=-mR^\top\mathbf p^{(4)}-\mathbf n
\end{equation}
and we can write:\begin{align}
    &\ddot T = \mu_3\\ 
    &\tau_\vartheta=\frac{I_y}{T}\mu_1\\ 
    &\tau_\varphi = -\frac{I_x}{T}\mu_2\\ 
    &\tau_\psi=\frac{I_zc_\vartheta}{c_\varphi}(\ddot\psi-I_\psi)-\frac{I_z}{I_y}\tan\varphi\tau_\vartheta
\end{align}
now that we have defined the map $\mathbf u=\Psi(\mathbf y,\dot{\mathbf y},\dots, \mathbf y^{(4)})$, we can define any trajectory at least four times differentiable $\mathbf y(t)$ and get immediately through $\Psi$ the required inputs to execute $\mathbf y(t)$.
\section{Geometric Control on $SO(3)$}
The state space of the rotational dynamics is $\R^3\times SO(3)$, this is a \textbf{topological obstruction}: due to this, it is impossible with a continuous feedback law to make an equilibrium point \textit{globally asymptotically stable}. The best thing that we can do is to use the information about the geometry of the state space and define an error on that. By doing so, the best achievable result with a continuous feedback law is \textbf{almost global convergence} (a desired equilibrium attracts trajectories starting from almost all initial conditions, except a zero-measure set). The \textbf{geometric controller} is designed by using directly $SO(3)$ to represent the desired attitude of the UAV.\bigskip

\noindent We recall the quadrotor dynamics:\begin{align}
    &\ddot{\mathbf p}=g\mathbf e_3-\frac{T}{m}R\mathbf e_3\\
    &\dot R= RS(\Bomega)\\
    &\label{JOmegadot}J\dot{\Bomega}+\Bomega\times J\Bomega=\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
\end{align}
with $\boldsymbol{\Gamma},T$ being the control inputs. We define a new operator $^\vee$: this is the inverse of the operator that assigns to a vector its associated skew-symmetric matrix:\begin{align}
    &A=S(\mathbf a)\\
    &A^\vee=(S(\mathbf a))^\vee=\mathbf a
\end{align}
The controller is divided in two loops, the outer loop for the position, the inner loop for the attitude. We denote $\mathbf b_1,\mathbf b_2,\mathbf b_3$ the unit vector columns of $R$ (these vectors are the one denoted $\mathbf x_b,\mathbf y_b,\mathbf z_b$ in Figure \ref{bodyframe}). To impose a desired attitude, we define a desired rotation matrix $R_d$, with column vectors $\mathbf b_{1d},\mathbf b_{2d},\mathbf b_{3d}$. The two loops work as follows:\begin{itemize}
    \item The trajectory tracking controller takes in input a desired Cartesian position $\mathbf p_d$, and produces in output the required thrust intensity $T$ and thrust direction $\mathbf b_{3d}$.
    \begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.25\textwidth ]{images/required_thrust.eps}
    \end{figure}
    \item $\mathbf b_{3d}$, and $\mathbf b_{1d}$ (desired yaw) are used to define $R_d$, that will be used by the attitude tracking controller to compute the required torques $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$.
    \item $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$ and $T$ are the computed control inputs for the quadrotor dynamics.
\end{itemize}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth ]{images/SE3_blockDiagram.eps}
    \caption{Block diagram of the geometric controller.}
\end{figure}
First of all we define the Cartesian error for the position:\begin{align}
    &\mathbf e_p=\mathbf p-\mathbf p_d\\
    &{\mathbf e}_v=\dot{\mathbf p}-\dot{\mathbf p}_d
\end{align}
the resulting positional control action $\mathbf a$ is built with a PD law + gravity compensation + feedforward:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf a=-k_p\mathbf e_p-k_d\mathbf e_v-mg\mathbf e_3+m\ddot{\mathbf p}_d.
\end{equation}
The thrust force that we can impose is $-TR\mathbf e_3$, and we would like to make this equal to the positional control action:\begin{equation}
    -TR\mathbf e_3=\mathbf a
\end{equation}
we cannot assign directly $-TR\mathbf e_3$, but we can assign $T$ and the control torques to control $R$. The thrust direction desired is the following:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf b_{3d}=-\frac{\mathbf a}{\|\mathbf a\|}.
\end{equation}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.25\textwidth ]{images/required_thrust2.eps}
    \end{figure}
\noindent This requires $\mathbf a\ne\mathbf 0$ (no free fall). The thrust intensity is:\begin{equation}
    T=\|\mathbf a\|\mathbf b_{3d}^\top\mathbf b_3
\end{equation}
so $T$ is the thrust intensity to actuate, now we have to describe how the inner attitude controller will achieve the desired attitude $R_d$ defined by the desired thrust direction $\mathbf b_{3d}$. The attitude is constructed as follows:\begin{itemize}
    \item Let $\mathbf b_{1d}$ be the desired yaw. We build the vector:\begin{equation}
        \mathbf b_{2d}=\frac{\mathbf b_{3d}\times\mathbf b_{1d}}{\|\mathbf b_{3d}\times\mathbf b_{1d}\|}
    \end{equation}
    \item $\mathbf b_{2d}$ and $\mathbf b_{3d}$ will be completed to an orthonormal basis by their cross product:\begin{equation}
        \mathbf b_{2d}\times \mathbf b_{3d}
    \end{equation}
    by this construction, $\mathbf b_{2d}\times \mathbf b_{3d}$ will be the projection of $\mathbf b_{1d}$ on the plane orthogonal to $\mathbf b_{3d}$. Ultimately:\begin{equation}
        R_d=\begin{pmatrix}
            &&\\ 
            \mathbf b_{2d}\times \mathbf b_{3d}&\mathbf b_{2d}&\mathbf b_{3d}\\
            &&
        \end{pmatrix}.
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth ]{images/R_d.eps}
\end{figure}
This construction is well defined if $\mathbf b_{1d}$ is not parallel to $\mathbf b_{3d}$. Now we have to define an error between matrices in $SO(3)$, so we define the error function $\Psi:SO(3)\times SO(3)\rightarrow [0,2]$ as follows:\begin{equation}
    \Psi(R,R_d)=\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left[I-R_d^\top R\right]
\end{equation}
this function has some important properties:\begin{itemize}
    \item $R_d^\top R$ is the relative rotation between the current and desired attitude.
    \item Let  $R_d^\top R$ be the rotation about $\mathbf r$ of $\theta$ radians (axis-angle representation). Then:\begin{equation}
        \text{tr}\left[R_d^\top R\right]=1+2\cos\theta
    \end{equation}
    hence:\begin{equation}
        \Psi(R,R_d)=1-\cos\theta\in [0,2].
    \end{equation}
    \item The error is zero if the matrices are identical:\begin{equation}
        \Psi(R,R_d)=0\iff R=R_d.
    \end{equation}
    \item The error is less than 1 if and only if the angle $\theta$ is less than $\frac{\pi}{2}$:\begin{equation}
         \Psi(R,R_d)<1\iff \theta<\frac{\pi}{2}.
    \end{equation}
    \item The error is maximum when the angle $\theta$ is exactly $\pi$:\begin{equation}
        \Psi(R,R_d)=2\iff \theta=\pi.
    \end{equation}
\end{itemize}
\begin{boxproposition}{Almost Global Convergence}{}
At the beginning of this section we have said that the geometric controller achieves almost global convergence. This means that asymptotic convergence is \textbf{guaranteed} if the error defined by $\Psi$ between the current attitude $R$ and desired attitude $R_d$ is less than 2.
\end{boxproposition}
At each attitude $R$, asymptotic convergence is guaranteed on the subset:\begin{equation}
    L_2=\{R_d\in SO(3) \ \text{ such that } \ \Psi(R,R_d)<2\}\subset SO(3).
\end{equation}
$L_2$  almost covers $SO(3)$, meaning that the set $SO(3)\setminus L_2$ has zero measure.\bigskip

\noindent Now that we defined a desired attitude $R_d$ and an error $\Psi(R,R_d)$ that is a measure of how distant we are from the desired configuration, we have to define how we choose the control torques $\boldsymbol\Gamma$. First of all we consider a \textit{small perturbation} on $R$, let $\boldsymbol{\eta}$ be a vector of infinitesimal rotation, the perturbation is:\begin{equation}
    \delta R= R S(\boldsymbol{\eta})
\end{equation}
now we compute how $\Psi$ changes when $R$ changes (and $R_d$ is constant). Since the trace operator is linear, the variation is pushed inside the operator:\begin{align}
    \delta\Psi&=\delta\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left[I-R_d^\top R\right]\\ 
    &=\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left[\delta I-\delta(R_d^\top R)\right]\\
    &=\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left[\delta I-(R_d^\top\delta R)\right]
\end{align}
$I$ is constant hence $\delta I$ is the zero matrix, then:\begin{equation}
     \delta\Psi=-\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left[R_d^\top R S(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right]
\end{equation}
from a series of algebraic steps (that we omit), the variation can be rewritten as follows:
\begin{align}
     \delta\Psi&=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\cdot \left(
        R_d^\top R-R^\top R_d
     \right)^\vee}_{\mathbf e_R}\cdot\boldsymbol{\eta}\\
     &=\mathbf e_R^\top \boldsymbol{\eta}
\end{align}
we have found the vector $\mathbf e_R$ that is the \textbf{gradient} of $\Psi$, represents the direction (expressed in the body frame) along which the error function $\Psi$ grows most rapidly. It can be proved that, if $\mathbf r,\theta$ is the axis-angle representation of the relative rotation $R_d^\top R$, then the gradient can be written as:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf e_R=\sin\theta\mathbf r
\end{equation}
furthermore its squared magnitude is:\begin{equation}
    \|\mathbf e_R\|^2=\underbrace{\sin^2\theta\mathbf r^\top \mathbf r=\sin^2\theta}_{\mathbf r \text{ is a unit vector}}=\Psi(2-\Psi).
\end{equation}
Let $\psi_2< 2$ be a constant value decided a priori by design, it holds that, for any attitude $R$ such that $\Psi(R,R_d)\le \psi_2$, the following bounds hold:\begin{equation}\label{bounds}
    \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf e_R\|^2\le\Psi\le\frac{1}{2-\psi_2}\|\mathbf e_R\|^2.
\end{equation}\begin{itemize}
    \item From the lower bound ($\frac{1}{2}\Vert{}\mathbf e_R\Vert{}^2 \le \Psi$) we deduce that if the configuration error $\Psi$ tends to zero, then the error vector $\mathbf e_R$ must also tend to zero ($\Psi \to 0 \implies \Vert{}\mathbf e_R\Vert{} \to 0$).
    \item From the upper bound ($\Psi \le \frac{1}{2-\psi_2}\Vert{}\mathbf e_R\Vert{}^2$) we deduce the vice versa: within the safe region $\Psi \le \psi_2 < 2$, if the error vector $\mathbf e_R$ vanishes, then $\Psi$  must also vanish, i.e. $R = R_d$.
\end{itemize}
\begin{boxobservation}{}{}
    The bounds on Equation \eqref{bounds} show that $\Psi$ is bounded between two quadratic forms of the attitude error vector $\mathbf{e}_R$; therefore, within this sublevel set, $\Psi(R, R_d)$ is positive definite with respect to $\mathbf{e}_R$ (i.e., $\Psi = 0 \iff \mathbf{e}_R = \mathbf{0}$).
\end{boxobservation}
After defining the attitude error, we define the error on angular velocity; let $\Bomega_d=(p_d,q_d,r_d)$ be the desired angular velocity, the error $\mathbf e_\Omega$ is:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf e_\Omega=\Bomega-R^\top R_d\Bomega_d.
\end{equation}
$\Bomega$ is expressed in the current body frame defined by $R$ while $\Bomega_d$ is expressed in the desired body frame defined by $R_d$, hence $R_d\Bomega_d$ is the desired angular velocity in the base frame (also called inertial frame). $\mathbf e_\Omega$ is exactly the angular velocity vector that governs the time derivative of the relative rotation matrix $R_d^\top R$:\begin{align}
    \frac{d}{dt}(R_d^\top R)&=\dot R_d^\top R+R_d^\top\dot R\\ 
    &=-S(\Bomega_d)R_d^\top R+R_d^\top RS(\Bomega)\\
    &=R_d^\top R(S(\Bomega)-R^\top R_d S(\Bomega_d)R_d^\top R)\\
    &=R_d^\top R\,S(\Bomega-R^\top R_d\Bomega_d)\\
    &=R_d^\top R\,S(\mathbf e_\Omega).
\end{align}
It can be proved that the derivative of the attitude error function is the dot product between the gradient and the angular velocity error:\begin{equation}
    \dot\Psi=-\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left[\frac{d}{dt}(R_d^\top R)\right]=
    -\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left[R_d^\top R S(\mathbf e_\Omega)\right]=
    \frac{1}{2}\mathbf e_\Omega^\top (R_d^\top R -R^\top R_d)^\vee=\mathbf e_R^\top\mathbf e_\Omega.
\end{equation}
Let's recap what we have done until now:\begin{enumerate}
    \item We defined the inner-outer loop structure of the geometric controller.
    \item We have seen how the outer loop (trajectory) generates the desired thrust direction for the inner loop, defined by $\mathbf b_{3d}$. 
    \item The inner loop constructs the matrix $R_d$, and defines an error $\Psi$ between the current attitude $R$ and the desired one $R_d$.
    \item We have defined the errors $\mathbf e_R=\nabla\Psi$ and $\mathbf e_\Omega$, and we have seen how $\dot\Psi=\mathbf e_R^\top\mathbf e_\Omega$.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{What we have to do now?}: We derive the dynamics of the errors, and choose the control torques $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$ in order to achieve a closed loop system that asymptotically nullifies the errors. \bigskip 

\noindent The dynamics of the attitude error is the following:\begin{align}
    \dot{\mathbf e}_R&=\frac{1}{2}\left(
        R_d^\top RS(\mathbf e_\Omega)+S(\mathbf e_\Omega)R^\top R_d 
    \right)^\vee\\
    &=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\left(
        \text{tr}\left[R^\top R_d\right]I-R^\top R_d
    \right)}_{C(R_d^\top R)}\mathbf e_\Omega
\end{align}
The matrix $\frac{1}{2}\left(
        \text{tr}\left[R^\top R_d\right]I-R^\top R_d
    \right)$ can be rewritten as $C(R_d^\top R)$ with $C$ being a matrix with norm less or equal than 1:\begin{align}
        &\dot{\mathbf e}_R=C(R_d^\top R)\mathbf e_\Omega,\quad\quad\|C\|\le 1.
    \end{align} 
So the velocity of the attitude error depends on the angular velocity error, its dynamics is:\begin{align}
    \dot{\mathbf e}_\Omega&=\dot\Bomega-\frac{d}{dt}(R^\top R_d\Bomega_d)\\
    &=\dot\Bomega+S(\Bomega)R^\top R_d\Bomega_d-R^\top R_d \underbrace{S(\Bomega_d)\Bomega_d}_{\mathbf 0}-R^\top R_d\dot\Bomega_d\\ 
    &=\dot\Bomega+S(\Bomega)R^\top R_d\Bomega_d-R^\top R_d\dot\Bomega_d
\end{align}
Let's multiply both sides by the quadrotor inertia matrix $J$:\begin{equation}
    J\dot{\mathbf e}_\Omega=
    J\dot\Bomega+J\left(S(\Bomega)R^\top R_d\Bomega_d-R^\top R_d\dot\Bomega_d\right)
\end{equation}
we substitute $J\dot\Bomega$ with the dynamics in Equation \eqref{JOmegadot}:
\begin{equation} J\dot{\mathbf e}_\Omega=
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}-\Bomega\times J\Bomega+J\left(S(\Bomega)R^\top R_d\Bomega_d-R^\top R_d\dot\Bomega_d\right).
\end{equation}
The control input that we choose to stabilize the system is the following:\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\Gamma}=
    \blueText{-k_R\mathbf e_R-k_\Omega\mathbf e_\Omega}+\sapText{\Bomega\times J\Bomega} - 
    \greenText{J(S(\Bomega)R^\top R_d\Bomega_d-R^\top R_d\dot{\Bomega}_d)}
\end{equation}
let's break it down:\begin{itemize}
    \item $\blueText{-k_R\mathbf e_R-k_\Omega\mathbf e_\Omega}$ is a PD action on the errors in $SO(3)$,
    \item $\sapText{\Bomega\times J\Bomega}$ to cancel the gyroscopic terms,
    \item $\greenText{J(S(\Bomega)R^\top R_d\Bomega_d-R^\top R_d\dot{\Bomega}_d)}$ is a feedforward action of the desired angular acceleration.
\end{itemize}
The closed loop dynamics achieved with this choice of $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}$ is the following:\begin{align}
    &J\dot{\mathbf e}_\Omega=-k_R\mathbf e_R-k_\Omega\mathbf e_\Omega\\ 
    &\dot{\mathbf e}_R=C(R_d^\top R)\mathbf e_\Omega\\
    &\frac{d}{dt}(R_d^\top R)=R_d^\top RS(\mathbf e_\Omega)
\end{align}
%================ CONTINUATION: replaces \todo{slide 04 pag 19} ================%
\begin{boxobservation}{}{}
The closed loop attitude dynamics is \textbf{autonomous} in the variables $(R_d^\top R,\mathbf e_\Omega)$: it does not depend on the translational motion of the vehicle, nor on the thrust $T$. This is what allows us to analyze the complete system as a \textit{cascade}: the attitude error subsystem is studied first, and then it is seen as an input for the position error subsystem.
\end{boxobservation}
\noindent Note that the hierarchical structure (outer loop for the position, inner loop for the attitude) is the same of the previous controllers, but here \textbf{no time-scale separation is assumed}: the stability will be proved for the complete closed loop.
\subsection{Stability of the Attitude Dynamics}
We study the stability of the attitude error dynamics with the following Lyapunov candidate, that combines the kinetic energy of the angular velocity error and the attitude error function:\begin{equation}
    V=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf e_\Omega^\top J\mathbf e_\Omega+k_R\Psi(R,R_d).
\end{equation}
By using the closed loop dynamics and $\dot\Psi=\mathbf e_R^\top\mathbf e_\Omega$ we get:\begin{align}
    \dot V&=\mathbf e_\Omega^\top J\dot{\mathbf e}_\Omega+k_R\dot\Psi\\
    &=\mathbf e_\Omega^\top(-k_R\mathbf e_R-k_\Omega\mathbf e_\Omega)+k_R\mathbf e_R^\top\mathbf e_\Omega\\
    &=-k_\Omega\|\mathbf e_\Omega\|^2\le0.
\end{align}
$\dot V$ is only negative semidefinite, but it is sufficient to prove an important property: $V$ is non-increasing along the trajectories, hence \begin{equation}
    k_R\Psi(t)\le V(t)\le V(0).
\end{equation}
If the initial condition is such that $V(0)<2k_R$, then $\Psi(t)<2$ for all $t\ge0$: \textbf{the attitude error never reaches the maximum value $2$}, i.e. the $180^\circ$ error (the undesired equilibria, where $\mathbf e_R=\mathbf 0$ but $R\ne R_d$). We can write the condition on $V(0)$ as a condition on the initial angular velocity error. Since $V(0)\le\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\max}(J)\|\mathbf e_\Omega(0)\|^2+k_R\Psi(0)$, the condition $V(0)<2k_R$ holds if\begin{equation}\label{initial_condition_omega}
    \|\mathbf e_\Omega(0)\|^2<\frac{2k_R}{\lambda_{\max}(J)}\left(2-\Psi(0)\right)
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\max}(J)$ is the maximum eigenvalue of $J$ (and $\lambda_{\min}(J)$ will be the minimum one).\bigskip

\noindent To prove \textit{exponential} stability we need a strict Lyapunov function, that we obtain by adding a cross term to $V$, with $c_2>0$ a constant to be chosen:\begin{equation}
    V_2=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf e_\Omega^\top J\mathbf e_\Omega+k_R\Psi+c_2\mathbf e_R^\top\mathbf e_\Omega.
\end{equation}
The derivative of the cross term is\begin{align}
    \frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf e_R^\top\mathbf e_\Omega\right)&=\dot{\mathbf e}_R^\top\mathbf e_\Omega+\mathbf e_R^\top\dot{\mathbf e}_\Omega\\
    &=(C(R_d^\top R)\mathbf e_\Omega)^\top\mathbf e_\Omega-\mathbf e_R^\top J^{-1}(k_R\mathbf e_R+k_\Omega\mathbf e_\Omega)\\
    &\le\|\mathbf e_\Omega\|^2-\frac{k_R}{\lambda_{\max}(J)}\|\mathbf e_R\|^2+\frac{k_\Omega}{\lambda_{\min}(J)}\|\mathbf e_R\|\|\mathbf e_\Omega\|
\end{align}
where we used $\|C\|\le1$. By summing it (multiplied by $c_2$) to $\dot V$ we obtain:\begin{equation}
    \dot V_2\le-(k_\Omega-c_2)\|\mathbf e_\Omega\|^2-\frac{c_2k_R}{\lambda_{\max}(J)}\|\mathbf e_R\|^2+\frac{c_2k_\Omega}{\lambda_{\min}(J)}\|\mathbf e_R\|\|\mathbf e_\Omega\|=-\mathbf z_2^\top W_2\mathbf z_2
\end{equation}
with\begin{equation}
    \mathbf z_2=\begin{pmatrix}
        \|\mathbf e_R\|\\ \|\mathbf e_\Omega\|
    \end{pmatrix},\quad W_2=\begin{pmatrix}
        \frac{c_2k_R}{\lambda_{\max}(J)}&-\frac{c_2k_\Omega}{2\lambda_{\min}(J)}\\[1ex]
        -\frac{c_2k_\Omega}{2\lambda_{\min}(J)}&k_\Omega-c_2
    \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
$W_2$ is positive definite if $c_2$ is small enough. Furthermore, on the sublevel set $\{\Psi\le\psi_2<2\}$ the bounds in Equation \eqref{bounds} hold, hence $V_2$ is bounded between two quadratic forms of $\mathbf z_2$ (if $c_2$ is small enough), and $\dot V_2\le-\lambda_{\min}(W_2)\|\mathbf z_2\|^2$. This proves the following result.
\begin{boxproposition}{Exponential Stability of the Attitude Dynamics}{att_stability}
Consider the attitude tracking controller $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ defined above. If the initial errors satisfy\begin{align}
    &\Psi(R(0),R_d(0))<2\\
    &\|\mathbf e_\Omega(0)\|^2<\frac{2k_R}{\lambda_{\max}(J)}\left(2-\Psi(R(0),R_d(0))\right)
\end{align}
then the zero equilibrium of the attitude tracking errors $(\mathbf e_R,\mathbf e_\Omega)$ is \textbf{exponentially stable}: $\mathbf e_R,\mathbf e_\Omega\to\mathbf 0$ exponentially.
\end{boxproposition}
\noindent In words: the attitude error converges exponentially to zero for any initial attitude error smaller than $180^\circ$ ($\Psi<2$), provided that the initial angular velocity error is not too large.
\subsection{Position Error Dynamics}
We now study the translational dynamics. Recall that $\mathbf e_p=\mathbf p-\mathbf p_d$ and $\mathbf e_v=\dot{\mathbf p}-\dot{\mathbf p}_d$, hence\begin{equation}
    m\dot{\mathbf e}_v=mg\mathbf e_3-TR\mathbf e_3-m\ddot{\mathbf p}_d.
\end{equation}
If the thrust force $-TR\mathbf e_3$ were exactly equal to the positional control action $\mathbf a$, we would get $m\dot{\mathbf e}_v=-k_p\mathbf e_p-k_d\mathbf e_v$, i.e. a stable linear error dynamics. Since this is not the case when $\mathbf b_3\ne\mathbf b_{3d}$, we add and subtract the force along the desired direction $\mathbf b_{3d}$:\begin{equation}
    -TR\mathbf e_3=-\frac{T}{\mathbf b_{3d}^\top\mathbf b_3}\mathbf b_{3d}-\mathbf X,\quad\quad
    \mathbf X:=\frac{T}{\mathbf b_{3d}^\top\mathbf b_3}\left((\mathbf b_{3d}^\top\mathbf b_3)\mathbf b_3-\mathbf b_{3d}\right).
\end{equation}
Since $T=\|\mathbf a\|(\mathbf b_{3d}^\top\mathbf b_3)$ and $-\|\mathbf a\|\mathbf b_{3d}=\mathbf a$, the first term is exactly $\mathbf a$, and the position error dynamics becomes:\begin{equation}\label{position_error_dynamics}
    m\dot{\mathbf e}_v=-k_p\mathbf e_p-k_d\mathbf e_v-\mathbf X.
\end{equation}
The vector $\mathbf X$ is the effect of the attitude error on the translational dynamics. Some properties:\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathbf X=\mathbf 0$ when $\mathbf b_3=\mathbf b_{3d}$: \textbf{if there is no attitude error, the position error converges} (linear dynamics in Equation \eqref{position_error_dynamics}).
    \item Let $\alpha$ be the angle between $\mathbf b_3$ and $\mathbf b_{3d}$. Then $\|(\mathbf b_{3d}^\top\mathbf b_3)\mathbf b_3-\mathbf b_{3d}\|=\sin\alpha$ and, since the thrust axes are the third columns of $R$ and $R_d$, $\alpha\le\theta$ where $\theta$ is the angle of the relative rotation $R_d^\top R$. 
    \item For $\Psi<1$ ($\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}$) it holds $\sin\alpha\le\sin\theta=\|\mathbf e_R\|$, and $\|\mathbf a\|\le k_p\|\mathbf e_p\|+k_d\|\mathbf e_v\|+\mathcal B$, hence\begin{equation}\label{bound_X}
        \|\mathbf X\|=\|\mathbf a\|\sin\alpha\le\left(k_p\|\mathbf e_p\|+k_d\|\mathbf e_v\|+\mathcal B\right)\|\mathbf e_R\|.
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathcal B$ is the bound on the reference, $\|-mg\mathbf e_3+m\ddot{\mathbf p}_d\|\le\mathcal B$ (we use $\mathcal B$ in order to avoid confusion with the matrix $B$ of the DFL).  Moreover $\|\mathbf e_R\|\le\sqrt{\psi_1(2-\psi_1)}<1$ on the set $\{\Psi\le\psi_1<1\}$.
    \item For $\Psi<1$ we have also $\alpha<\frac{\pi}{2}$, hence $T=\|\mathbf a\|\cos\alpha>0$: the dot product in the definition of $T$ \textbf{reduces the thrust when the two axes separate}, and keeps it positive in the region where the analysis of the complete system holds.
\end{itemize}
The assumptions are $\|\mathbf a\|\ne0$ (no free fall, the analogue of $T\ne0$ in the DFL) and a bounded reference $\|-mg\mathbf e_3+m\ddot{\mathbf p}_d\|\le\mathcal B$.
\subsection{Stability of the Complete System}
The structure of the complete error dynamics is a cascade: the attitude error subsystem $(\mathbf e_R,\mathbf e_\Omega)$ is autonomous, and it drives the position error subsystem $(\mathbf e_p,\mathbf e_v)$ through the term $\mathbf X$. For the position part we choose the following Lyapunov function, with $c_1>0$ a constant to be chosen:\begin{equation}
    V_1=\frac{1}{2}k_p\|\mathbf e_p\|^2+\frac{1}{2}m\|\mathbf e_v\|^2+c_1\mathbf e_p^\top\mathbf e_v
\end{equation}
and for the complete system $V=V_1+V_2$. By deriving $V_1$ along Equation \eqref{position_error_dynamics} we get:\begin{equation}
    \dot V_1\le-\frac{c_1k_p}{m}\|\mathbf e_p\|^2-(k_d-c_1)\|\mathbf e_v\|^2+\frac{c_1k_d}{m}\|\mathbf e_p\|\|\mathbf e_v\|+\|\mathbf X\|\left(\|\mathbf e_v\|+\frac{c_1}{m}\|\mathbf e_p\|\right).
\end{equation}
The first three terms are the ones of the nominal system (without attitude error). The last term is bounded with Equation \eqref{bound_X}, and it is a term of the form $\|\mathbf e_R\|\cdot(\dots)$. On the domain $\|\mathbf e_p\|<e_{p,\max}$ the bound is a quadratic form, and we obtain:\begin{equation}
    \dot V\le-\mathbf z_1^\top W_1\mathbf z_1+\mathbf z_1^\top W_{12}\mathbf z_2-\mathbf z_2^\top W_2\mathbf z_2,\quad\quad \mathbf z_1=\begin{pmatrix}
        \|\mathbf e_p\|\\ \|\mathbf e_v\|
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
with\begin{equation}
    W_1=\begin{pmatrix}
        \frac{c_1k_p}{m}&-\frac{c_1k_d}{2m}\\[1ex]
        -\frac{c_1k_d}{2m}&k_d-c_1
    \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
and $W_{12}$ collects the bound on $\mathbf X$ (it depends on $\mathcal B$, $e_{p,\max}$, $k_p,k_d$ and $c_1$). We have\begin{equation}
    \dot V\le-\lambda_{\min}(W_1)\|\mathbf z_1\|^2+\|W_{12}\|\|\mathbf z_1\|\|\mathbf z_2\|-\lambda_{\min}(W_2)\|\mathbf z_2\|^2
\end{equation}
that is negative definite if and only if (discriminant of the quadratic form):\begin{equation}
    4\lambda_{\min}(W_1)\lambda_{\min}(W_2)>\|W_{12}\|^2.
\end{equation}
This is a condition on the attitude gains $k_R,k_\Omega$ (that appear in $W_2$) with respect to the position gains: \textbf{the attitude gains must be large enough with respect to the position loop}. It is a provable version of the rule ``the inner loop must be faster than the outer loop'' that we mentioned for the hierarchical control scheme.
\begin{boxproposition}{Exponential Stability of the Complete System}{complete_stability}
For any positive $k_p,k_d$ there exist $c_1,c_2,k_R,k_\Omega$ such that, if \begin{align}
    &\Psi(R(0),R_d(0))<\psi_1<1\\
    &\|\mathbf e_p(0)\|<e_{p,\max}\\
    &\|\mathbf e_\Omega(0)\|\ \text{is small enough}
\end{align}
the zero equilibrium of the tracking errors $(\mathbf e_p,\mathbf e_v,\mathbf e_R,\mathbf e_\Omega)$ of the complete system is \textbf{exponentially stable}. This requires an initial attitude error smaller than $90^\circ$.
\end{boxproposition}
\noindent The region of Proposition \ref{def:complete_stability} is smaller than the one of Proposition \ref{def:att_stability}. Let's see how we can extend the result to initial attitude errors up to $180^\circ$.
\begin{boxproposition}{Almost Global Exponential Attractiveness}{almost_global}
If $\Psi(R(0),R_d(0))<2$ and \begin{equation}
    \|\mathbf e_\Omega(0)\|^2<\frac{2k_R}{\lambda_{\max}(J)}\left(2-\Psi(R(0),R_d(0))\right)
\end{equation}
then the zero equilibrium of the tracking errors of the complete system is \textbf{exponentially attractive}.
\end{boxproposition}
\noindent The idea of the proof is the following:\begin{enumerate}
    \item Since the attitude subsystem is autonomous, from any $\Psi(0)<2$ (with the bound on $\mathbf e_\Omega(0)$) the attitude error decreases exponentially and enters the set $\{\Psi\le\psi_1\}$ at a finite time $t^*$.
    \item On the interval $[0,t^*]$ the position errors remain bounded: $\|\mathbf X\|$ grows at most linearly with $\|\mathbf e_p\|,\|\mathbf e_v\|$ (by Equation \eqref{bound_X} with $\sin\alpha\le1$), hence the system of Equation \eqref{position_error_dynamics} is a linear system with a bounded-gain input, and there is \textbf{no finite escape time}.
    \item After $t^*$, Proposition \ref{def:complete_stability} applies.
\end{enumerate}
$L_2$ almost covers $SO(3)$, hence this is an \textbf{almost global exponential attractiveness}, that is the best result achievable with a continuous feedback law, because of the topological obstruction. The following table summarizes the stability properties of the controller:
\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
    \begin{tabular}{lll}
        \toprule
        \textsf{\textbf{initial attitude error}} & \textsf{\textbf{subsystem}} & \textsf{\textbf{property}} \\
        \midrule
        $\Psi<2$ ($\theta<180^\circ$) & attitude & exponential stability \\
        $\Psi\le\psi_1<1$ ($\theta<90^\circ$) & complete & exponential stability \\
        $\Psi<2$ ($\theta<180^\circ$) & complete & exponential attractiveness \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}
\begin{boxobservation}{}{}
Almost global convergence is a statement about the \textit{basin of attraction}, not about the \textit{performance}. Near the $180^\circ$ error we have $\|\mathbf e_R\|=\sin\theta\simeq0$, hence the torque $-k_R\mathbf e_R$ is very small and the error leaves this region very slowly.
\end{boxobservation}
\subsection{Computation of the Desired Angular Velocity and Acceleration}
The attitude controller requires $\Bomega_d$ and $\dot\Bomega_d$, that are defined through the desired attitude $R_d(t)$:\begin{align}
    S(\Bomega_d)&=R_d^\top\dot R_d\\
    S(\dot\Bomega_d)&=R_d^\top\ddot R_d-S(\Bomega_d)^2.
\end{align}
The second equation follows from the derivative of the first one: $\frac{d}{dt}(R_d^\top\dot R_d)=\dot R_d^\top\dot R_d+R_d^\top\ddot R_d$, and $\dot R_d^\top\dot R_d=S(\Bomega_d)^\top R_d^\top R_dS(\Bomega_d)=-S(\Bomega_d)^2$.\bigskip

\noindent $R_d$ depends on $\mathbf b_{1d}(t)$ and on $\mathbf a(\mathbf e_p,\mathbf e_v,\ddot{\mathbf p}_d)$, hence we have to compute the derivatives of $\mathbf a$. We want to avoid the numerical differentiation of the measurements, so we use the model:\begin{align}
    \dot{\mathbf e}_v&=g\mathbf e_3-\frac{T}{m}R\mathbf e_3-\ddot{\mathbf p}_d\\
    \dot{\mathbf a}&=-k_p\mathbf e_v-k_d\dot{\mathbf e}_v+m\mathbf p_d^{(3)}\\
    \dot T&=-\dot{\mathbf a}^\top R\mathbf e_3-\mathbf a^\top RS(\Bomega)\mathbf e_3\\
    \ddot{\mathbf e}_v&=-\frac{\dot T}{m}R\mathbf e_3-\frac{T}{m}RS(\Bomega)\mathbf e_3-\mathbf p_d^{(3)}\\
    \ddot{\mathbf a}&=-k_p\dot{\mathbf e}_v-k_d\ddot{\mathbf e}_v+m\mathbf p_d^{(4)}.
\end{align}
where $\dot T$ comes from the derivative of $T=-\mathbf a^\top R\mathbf e_3$. Now we need the derivatives of $\mathbf b_{3d}$, $\mathbf b_{2d}$ and then of $R_d$. Both are obtained by normalizing a vector. For a generic vector $\mathbf h$ and the unit vector $\mathbf s=\frac{\mathbf h}{\|\mathbf h\|}$:\begin{align}
    \dot{\mathbf s}&=\frac{\dot{\mathbf h}-\mathbf s(\mathbf s^\top\dot{\mathbf h})}{\|\mathbf h\|}\\
    \ddot{\mathbf s}&=\frac{\ddot{\mathbf h}-2\dot{\mathbf s}(\mathbf s^\top\dot{\mathbf h})-\mathbf s(\dot{\mathbf s}^\top\dot{\mathbf h}+\mathbf s^\top\ddot{\mathbf h})}{\|\mathbf h\|}.
\end{align}
We apply these formulas first to $\mathbf b_{3d}$ (with $\mathbf h=-\mathbf a$), then to $\mathbf b_{2d}$ (with $\mathbf h=\mathbf b_{3d}\times\mathbf b_{1d}$), and the product rule on $\mathbf b_{2d}\times\mathbf b_{3d}$, which gives $\dot R_d$ and $\ddot R_d$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item This requires $\mathbf p_d\in C^4$ (up to the snap) and $\mathbf b_{1d}\in C^2$: the same smoothness required for the flat outputs in the DFL.
    \item $\ddot{\mathbf a}$ depends on the state (through $\Bomega$) but not on the control torque $\boldsymbol\Gamma$: there is no algebraic loop.
    \item In many implementations $\dot\Bomega_d$ is neglected or obtained through a filtered differentiation: in this case the exact cancellation of the feedforward term, and hence the proof, hold only approximately.
\end{itemize}
\noindent Let's discuss about some \textit{implementation remarks}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sign of the thrust}: $T=\|\mathbf a\|\cos\alpha<0$ when the thrust axis is more than $90^\circ$ away from $\mathbf b_{3d}$, hence the control law assumes a \textit{reversible} thrust. With real propellers $T$ is saturated in $[0,T_{\max}]$; in the region $\{\Psi<1\}$ we have $T>0$ anyway. Since the attitude error dynamics do not depend on $T$, the attitude still converges, and the position recovers afterwards.
    \item \textbf{Singular situations} appear only in the reference generation:\begin{itemize}
        \item $\|\mathbf a\|=0$: commanded free fall, $\mathbf b_{3d}$ is undefined (the analogue of $T=0$ in the DFL);
        \item $\mathbf b_{3d}\parallel\mathbf b_{1d}$: the desired heading is undefined, it can be avoided by changing $\mathbf b_{1d}$.
    \end{itemize}
    \item The controller is a \textbf{static} feedback: there is no compensator state to initialize (in the DFL we have $\bzeta=(T,\dot T)$).
    \item Numerical example: $m=4.34\,\text{kg}$, $J=\text{diag}(0.082,0.0845,0.1377)\,\text{kg}\,\text{m}^2$, $k_p=16m$, $k_d=5.6m$, $k_R=8.81$, $k_\Omega=2.54$.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Comparison Between DFL and Geometric Control}
We compare the complete DFL controller with the geometric controller on $SE(3)$ in simulation, by using the same trajectories and initial conditions.\bigskip

\noindent \textbf{Simulation 1}: tracking of an 8-shaped trajectory while spinning, i.e. far from the singularities:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf p_d=\begin{pmatrix}
        2\sin\frac{2\pi}{T}t&\sin2\frac{2\pi}{T}t&0.6\sin\frac{2\pi}{T}t+\sin2\frac{2\pi}{T}t
    \end{pmatrix}^\top,\quad\quad\psi_d=\pi\sin\frac{2\pi}{T}t
\end{equation}
(here $T$ is the period of the trajectory, not the thrust). The attitude error function of the $SE(3)$ controller is $\Psi(R,R_d)=\frac{1}{2}\text{tr}\left[I-R_d^\top R\right]$, and it converges to zero in about one second.\begin{itemize}
    \item The two controllers show different transients in position tracking, then both converge to the reference.
    \item The DFL has a faster convergence for a comparable control effort.
    \item The $SE(3)$ controller tracks only the \textit{projection} of $\mathbf b_{1d}$ on the plane orthogonal to $\mathbf b_{3d}$: its RPY yaw angle differs from $\psi_d$ when the vehicle is tilted. The DFL tracks exactly the yaw angle $\psi_d$.
\end{itemize}
\noindent \textbf{Simulation 2}: tracking of a helix with a constant yaw angle:\begin{equation}
    \mathbf p_d=\begin{pmatrix}
        0.2t&\sin\frac{2\pi}{T}t&\cos\frac{2\pi}{T}t
    \end{pmatrix}^\top,\quad\quad\psi_d=0.
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The complete DFL \textbf{runs into the singularity} $\vartheta=-\frac{\pi}{2}$ at $t\simeq0.72$ s.
    \item Here $\det M(\bTheta)=\cos\vartheta\to0$: this is the gimbal lock of the RPY angles. The yaw row of the matrix $D$ contains $\frac{1}{c_\vartheta}$, hence the control law is not defined and the angular rates diverge.
    \item With the same trajectory and the same initial conditions, the $SE(3)$ controller converges: $\Psi\to0$ in about $1$ s, and the position error converges after the transient.
\end{itemize}

\noindent To summarize:\begin{itemize}
    \item Due to the occurrence of singularities in the DFL, the $SE(3)$ controller is more \textbf{reliable}, in particular for large attitude excursions.
    \item When no singularity occurs, no significant difference can be appreciated between the two controllers.
    \item The control effort of the DFL becomes huge when the determinant of the decoupling matrix $D$ approaches $0$ (or diverges, at $\vartheta=\pm\frac{\pi}{2}$).
    \item The complete DFL is a linear controller for a fully linearized system: far from the singularities the convergence is exponential. The convergence of the complete dynamics of the $SE(3)$ controller becomes exponential only when the attitude error is below $90^\circ$.
    \item Both controllers need the reference up to the snap and an accurate model of the vehicle.
\end{itemize}
